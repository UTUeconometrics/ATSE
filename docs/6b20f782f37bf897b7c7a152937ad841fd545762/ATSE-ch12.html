12  Big Dependent Data

12.1 Background

So far, we have examined standard VAR model (and its VECM counterpart). In practice, the number of variables in these models must be kept relatively small to maintain estimation accuracy and efficiency. Models with more than, say, six variables are rare. However, especially in many economic applications, this dimensionality constraint presents significant challenges.

Omitted variable bias. In applications where the underlying dynamics are driven by a large information set, restricting the model to a handful of variables creates a substantial risk of omitted variable bias.

  • For example, central banks monitor hundreds of variables when conducting monetary policy. Excluding this information from a model can lead to distorted results.

  • A classic example is the “price puzzle” observed in early VAR-based monetary policy literature, where a contractionary monetary policy shock appeared to increase the price level. This counter-intuitive result is widely attributed to omitted variable bias: if the model does not include forward-looking inflation expectations (which the central bank observes), it misinterprets a policy rate hike—enacted in anticipation of future inflation—as the cause of the subsequent price rise.

Unobserved variables. Economic theory or other background information (or equivalent results in other application fields) often features variables that lack precise empirical counterparts or are measured with error.

  • For instance, “inflation” is a theoretical concept representing the decline in the value of money. In practice, we observe proxies like the Consumer Price Index (CPI) or core inflation (excluding volatile energy and food prices).

  • It is often unclear which specific proxy best represents the underlying theoretical variable. A factor model approach allows us to extract the common signal (the underlying inflation) from a large panel of different price indicators.

Modelling specific dynamics. Standard VARs focus on varying a few key aggregates. However, researchers are often interested in the dynamic reaction of specific, disaggregated variables (e.g., sector-specific prices or regional housing starts). Including all these variables in a standard VAR is computationally infeasible.

 

The FAVAR solution. One solution to these dimensionality problems, within the VAR context, is the Factor Augmented Vector Autoregression (FAVAR) model. The core idea is to utilize a large dataset (where even the number of variables \(M\) may exceed the number of observations \(T\)) to construct a small number of common factors via Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and then include these factors in the VAR model.

The FAVAR approach offers distinct advantages:

  1. Dimension reduction: It condenses the information from hundreds of variables into a few factors, keeping the parameter space manageable.

  2. Proxies for unobservables: Factors can act as better proxies for theoretical concepts (like “economic activity” or “global financial cycle”) than any single observed variable.

  3. Broader analysis: It allows for the analysis of shocks (in structural VARs) on a vast set of variables that are not explicitly in the VAR system but are part of the large dataset used to extract factors.

In the following sections, we will focus first on extracting factors from large datasets and second on utilizing them in the VAR analysis. This discussion draws heavily on Chapter 16 of Kilian and Lütkepohl (2017).

Note: The notation in this chapter differs slightly from previous sections. Specifically, matrices and dimensions here relate to large-scale factor methods and should not be confused, for example, with notation in the previous cointegration and VECM section.

 

12.2 Factor models

Static factor model

Let \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) be an \(M \times 1\) vector of observed, demeaned variables. We assume these variables are driven by a small number of unobserved common factors \(\boldsymbol{f}_t\) (\(r \times 1\)) and idiosyncratic errors. The static factor model is defined as: \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \boldsymbol{f}_t + \boldsymbol{v}_t, \end{equation*}\] where

  • \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) is the (\(M \times r\)) matrix of factor loadings.

  • \(\boldsymbol{v}_t \sim \mathsf{iid}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_v)\) is the vector of idiosyncratic errors.

We assume that the common factors and idiosyncratic errors are uncorrelated, i.e., \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{v}_s \boldsymbol{f}_t^\prime) = \boldsymbol{0}\) for all \(s, t\). Typically, we assume \(M \gg r\). The covariance matrix of \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) implies: \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_x = \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_f \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^\prime + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_v. \end{equation*}\] This model is not uniquely identified without further restrictions. Common identification assumptions include:

  1. Orthogonal loadings: \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^\prime \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \boldsymbol{I}_r\).

  2. Uncorrelated, standardized factors: \(\boldsymbol{f}_t \sim (\boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{I}_r)\), implying \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_f\) is a diagonal matrix.

 

Estimation via PCA

If \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) were known, we could estimate the factors easily. In practice, we estimate the factors by maximizing the variance of \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) explained by the common component (equivalently, minimizing the variance of the idiosyncratic errors). The objective function is: \[\begin{equation*} \min_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}, \boldsymbol{f}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_T} \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_t-\boldsymbol{\Lambda} \boldsymbol{f}_t)^\prime (\boldsymbol{x}_t-\boldsymbol{\Lambda} \boldsymbol{f}_t). \end{equation*}\] The solution is found via Principal Component Analysis (PCA):

  1. Find the \(r\) largest eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 > \dots > \lambda_r\) of the sample covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{S}_x = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_t \boldsymbol{x}_t^\prime\) and their corresponding (orthonormal) eigenvectors \(\bar{\lambda}_1, \dots, \bar{\lambda}_r\).

  2. Set the estimated loading matrix is \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}} = [\bar{\lambda}_1, \dots, \bar{\lambda}_r]\).

  3. The estimated factors are \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}_t = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^\prime \boldsymbol{x}_t\).

The eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) represent the empirical variance explained by the \(i\)-th factor, ordered from largest to smallest.

 

Dynamic factor model

The static model assumes factors and errors are serially uncorrelated. The Dynamic Factor Model (DFM) generalizes this by allowing for autoregressive dynamics: \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^f \boldsymbol{f}_t + \boldsymbol{v}_t, \end{equation*}\] where the factors and errors follow time-series processes: \[\begin{align*} \boldsymbol{f}_t &= \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_1 \boldsymbol{f}_{t-1} + \cdots + \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_s \boldsymbol{f}_{t-s} + \boldsymbol{\eta}_t, \\ \boldsymbol{v}_t &= \boldsymbol{A}_1 \boldsymbol{v}_{t-1} + \cdots + \boldsymbol{A}_p \boldsymbol{v}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{u}_t. \end{align*}\] Here \(\boldsymbol{A}_i\) are typically diagonal matrices, and \(\boldsymbol{u}_t\) is white noise (or iid) error term.

 

Note: We assume \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) is stationary. While factor models exist for integrated processes, the methods presented here assume stationarity.

 

Dynamic form. If \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) depends directly on lagged values of the factors, we obtain the dynamic form of the model: \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_0^f \boldsymbol{f}_t + \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_1^f \boldsymbol{f}_{t-1} + \cdots + \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{q^*}^f \boldsymbol{f}_{t-q^*} + \boldsymbol{v}_t. \end{equation*}\] Assuming the same DGP for \(\boldsymbol{f}_t\) and \(\boldsymbol{v}_t\) as in the static form (as above) of the DFM, this model can be written in lag operator notation as \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^f(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{f}_t + \boldsymbol{v}_t, \quad \boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{f}_t = \boldsymbol{\eta}_t, \quad \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{v}_t = \boldsymbol{u}_t \end{equation*}\] where \[\begin{eqnarray*} \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^f(\mathsf{B}) &=& \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_0^f + \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_1^f \mathsf{B} + \cdots + \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{q^*}^f \mathsf{B}^{q^*}, \\ \boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\mathsf{B}) &=& \boldsymbol{I}_r - \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_1 \mathsf{B}- \cdots - \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_s \mathsf{B}^s, \\ \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}) &=& \mathrm{diag}\Big[a_1(\mathsf{B}) \, \cdots \, a_M(\mathsf{B}) \Big], \end{eqnarray*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{v}_t\) is the vector of idiosyncratic components, and \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_t\) is white noise or iid error term such that \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_t \boldsymbol{\eta}^{\prime}_s) = \boldsymbol{0} \, \forall t,s\)).

Interestingly, this dynamic form can be rewritten in a static representation by stacking the current and lagged factors into a larger vector \(\boldsymbol{F}_t = (\boldsymbol{f}_t^\prime, \ldots, \boldsymbol{f}_{t-q^*}^\prime)\). The observation equation becomes \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{x}_t = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^F \boldsymbol{F}_t + \boldsymbol{v}_t, \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^F = \Big[\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_0^f \quad \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_1^f \quad \cdots \quad \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{q^*}^f \Big]\). This allows us to estimate the dynamic model using the PCA approach described for the static model.

Estimation strategy. Recall that the model can be rewritten as \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{x}_t = \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^f(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{f}_t + \boldsymbol{u}_t. \end{equation*}\] A recommended iterative procedure for estimating the DFM (see Stock and Watson, 2005):

  1. Initialize: Estimate univariate AR models for each component of \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) to get initial estimates \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{A}}(\mathsf{B})\).

  2. PCA step: Estimate factors \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_t\) from the filtered model \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{A}}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{x}_t\) using PCA.

  3. Regression step: Estimate the factor loadings and error dynamics by regressing \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) on the estimated factors.

  4. Iterate: Repeat steps 2 and 3 until convergence.

 

Choosing the number of factors. Since PCA does not rely on the time structure, a common heuristic for selecting \(r\) (the number of factors) is the scree plot approach or a variance threshold. One specifies a target fraction of total variance (e.g., 50% or 90%) and selects the minimum number of principal components required to explain that cumulative variance.

Asymptotic convergence. Standard theory suggests estimation improves as \(M \to \infty\). However, Boivin and Ng (2006) highlight that simply adding more data is not always beneficial if the new variables have correlated errors or weak factor loadings. Quality matters more than quantity; including noisy or irrelevant variables can degrade the precision of factor estimates.

 

12.3 FAVAR Model

We can now formally define the FAVAR model to address the omitted variable bias. Assume the “true” process (such as economy) is driven by both observable variables \(\boldsymbol{y}_t\) (\(K \times 1\)) and unobserved factors \(\boldsymbol{f}_t\) (\(r \times 1\)). The joint dynamics are given by a demeaned(!) VAR: \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{z}_t = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{f}_t \\ \boldsymbol{y}_t \end{pmatrix} = \sum_{j=1}^{p} \boldsymbol{A}^*_j \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{f}_{t-j} \\ \boldsymbol{y}_{t-j} \end{pmatrix} + \widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_t, \end{equation*}\] where the coefficient matrix is partitioned as: \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}_j^* = \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{A}^*_{j, r \times r} & \boldsymbol{A}^*_{j, r \times K} \\ \boldsymbol{A}^*_{j, K \times r} & \boldsymbol{A}^*_{j, K \times K} \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}\] If we were to estimate a standard VAR using only \(\boldsymbol{y}_t\), we would effectively be restricting \(\boldsymbol{A}^*_{j, K \times r} = \boldsymbol{0}\). If the factors indeed drive \(\boldsymbol{y}_t\), this restriction is invalid and leads to the omitted variable bias.

Estimation and inference. The FAVAR approach proceeds in two steps:

  1. Factor extraction: Estimate the factors \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}_t\) from a large dataset \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) using, for example, the PCA methods described above.

  2. VAR estimation: Treat the estimated factors as observed variables and estimate the augmented VAR:

\[\begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix} \widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}_t \\ \boldsymbol{y}_t \end{pmatrix} = \sum_{j=1}^{p} \boldsymbol{A}_j \begin{pmatrix} \widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t-j} \\ \boldsymbol{y}_{t-j} \end{pmatrix} + \boldsymbol{u}_t. \end{equation*}\]

 

Notes on inference: Because \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}_t\) are estimated regressors (“generated regressors”) rather than true observations, standard asymptotic standard errors for impulse responses are incorrect. In practice, bootstrap methods are used to compute valid confidence intervals for FAVAR analysis.
