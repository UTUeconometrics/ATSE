6  VAR(\(p\)) process

6.1 Definition

In the case of a multivariate time series \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=(y_{1t},...,y_{Kt})\), the AR(\(p\)) process/model is generalized to the vector autoregressive process of order \(p\), that is the VAR(\(p\)) process \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_{t} = \boldsymbol{\nu} + \boldsymbol{A}_{1} \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}+\cdots + \boldsymbol{A}_{p} \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p}+\boldsymbol{u}_{t},\quad \boldsymbol{u}_t\sim \mathsf{iid}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u), \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{A}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\) are fixed coefficient matrices (\(K\times K\)) and \(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{t}=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{K})\) is fixed (\(K \times 1\)) vector of constant (intercept) terms allowing for the possibility of a nonzero mean \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{y}_t) \neq \boldsymbol{0}\). Furthermore, \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}=(u_{1t},\ldots,u _{Kt})\) is an unobserved (\(K\)-dimensional, \(K \times 1\)) IID error term (or “shock” or “innovation”). Unless stated otherwise, we assume that the covariance matrix \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_t \boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}_t) = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) is positive definite (i.e., non-singular and invertible matrix), and due to the IID (or weaker white noise) assumption \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_t \boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}_s) = \boldsymbol{0}\) for \(s \neq t\).

  • In a VAR(\(p\)) process/model, or just VAR(\(p\)) or a vector autoregression, “V” stands for “vector”.

  • The idea of the VAR(\(p\)) model is to regress each component of \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\) on its own lags and on the lags of the other variables, as in a (multiple-equation) linear regression.

  • The model can describe lagged or dynamic dependencies among variables. For example, one may ask how \(y_{2t}\) affects the future path of \(y_{1t}\) and how \(y_{1t}\) affects the future path of \(y_{2t}\).

 

Example and a special case: Two-variable VAR(1) process. Let us start with considering a bivariate case (\(K=2\)). That is we have two time series \(y_{1t}\) and \(y_{2t}\). A bivariate (i.e., a two-variable) first-order VAR process (i.e., a VAR(1) process) is given as \[\begin{eqnarray*} y_{1t} &=& \nu_1 + a_{11} y_{1, t-1} + a_{12} y_{2, t-1} + u_{1t} \\ y_{2t} &=& \nu_2 + a_{21} y_{1, t-1} + a_{22} y_{2, t-1} + u_{2t}, \end{eqnarray*}\] where \(u_{1t}\) and \(u_{2t}\) are two error terms (independent of the history of \(y_{1t}\) and \(y_{2t}\)) that may be correlated.

In a matrix notation, the bivariate VAR(1) process can be written \[\begin{eqnarray*} \left[ \begin{array}{c} y_{1t} \\ y_{2t} \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \end{array} \right] + \left[ \begin{array}{cc} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} y_{1, t-1} \\ y_{2, t-1} \end{array} \right] + \left[ \begin{array}{c} u_{1t} \\ u_{2t} \end{array} \right]. \end{eqnarray*}\] Equivalently, \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{\nu} + \boldsymbol{A}_1 \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{u}_t, \end{equation*}\] where now \(\boldsymbol{y}_t = (y_{1t}, y_{2t})\), \(\boldsymbol{\nu} = (\nu_1, \nu_2)\), and \(\boldsymbol{u}_t = (u_{1t}, u_{2t})\). Recall that \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{y}_t) = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \mathsf{E}(y_{1t}) \\ \mathsf{E}(y_{2t}) \end{array} \right] \equiv \boldsymbol{\mu} = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \mu_{1} \\ \mu_{2} \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}\] Moreover, the assumption \(\boldsymbol{u}_t\sim \mathsf{iid}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)\) means here that \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_t) = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \mathsf{E}(u_{1t}) \\ \mathsf{E}(u_{2t}) \end{array} \right] = \boldsymbol{0} = \left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ 0 \end{array} \right], \end{equation*}\] and \[\begin{align*} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u &= \mathsf{E} \Big( (\boldsymbol{u}_t - \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_t)) (\boldsymbol{u}_t - \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_t))^{\prime} \Big) \\[10pt] &= \left[ \begin{array}{cc} \mathsf{Var}(u_{1t}) & \mathsf{Cov}(u_{1t}, u_{2t}) \\ \mathsf{Cov}(u_{2t}, u_{1t}) & \mathsf{Var}(u_{2t}) \end{array} \right]. \end{align*}\]

 

Companion form of the VAR(\(p\)) process. The VAR(\(p\)) process can also be rewritten using so called companion form \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{Y}_t = \boldsymbol{\ubar{\nu}} + \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{U}_t, \end{equation*}\] where \[\begin{equation*} \mathbf{A}= \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{A}_{1} & \boldsymbol{A}_{2} & \dots & \boldsymbol{A}_{p-1} & \boldsymbol{A}_{p} \\ \boldsymbol{I}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K & \dots & \boldsymbol{0}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K \\ \boldsymbol{0}_K & \boldsymbol{I}_K & \dots & \boldsymbol{0}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{0}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K & \dots & \boldsymbol{I}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K \end{bmatrix}, \quad (Kp\times Kp) \end{equation*}\] where \[\begin{eqnarray*} && \boldsymbol{Y}_t=[\boldsymbol{y}_t^\prime \dots \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p+1}^\prime]^\prime \quad (Kp\times 1), \quad \boldsymbol{\ubar{\nu}} =[\boldsymbol{\nu}^\prime \, \cdots \, \boldsymbol{0}^\prime]^\prime \quad (Kp\times 1) \quad \mathrm{and} \quad \\ && \boldsymbol{U}_t=[\boldsymbol{u}_t^\prime \,\, \boldsymbol{0}^\prime \, \cdots \, \boldsymbol{0}^\prime]^\prime \quad (Kp\times 1). \end{eqnarray*}\] In other words, we have the following presentation \[\begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{y}_t \\ \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p+1} \end{array}\right] = \left[ \begin{array}{ccccc} \boldsymbol{A}_1 & \boldsymbol{A}_2 & \cdots & \boldsymbol{A}_{p-1} & \boldsymbol{A}_p \\ \boldsymbol{I}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K & \cdots & \boldsymbol{0}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K \\ \boldsymbol{0}_K & \boldsymbol{I}_K & \cdots & \boldsymbol{0}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{0}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K & \cdots & \boldsymbol{I}_K & \boldsymbol{0}_K \\ \end{array}\right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1} \\ \boldsymbol{y}_{t-2} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p} \end{array}\right] + \left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{u}_t \\ \boldsymbol{0} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{0} \end{array}\right], \end{equation*}\] indicating that there holds equalities \(\boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{\nu} + \boldsymbol{A}_1 \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1} + \cdots + \boldsymbol{A}_p \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{u}_t\), \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1} = \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}\), and so on to \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t-p+1}= \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p+1}\). Given assumption \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\sim \mathsf{iid} (\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)\), it follows that \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{U}_{t}\sim\mathsf{iid}(\boldsymbol{\ubar{0}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_U) , \quad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_U = \mathsf{diag}[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \text{ }\boldsymbol{0} \text{ }\cdots \text{ } \boldsymbol{0}] \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_U = \mathsf{diag}[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_U \cdots \boldsymbol{0}]\) is a block diagonal matrix with the given (\(K \times K\)) matrices along the diagonal. The covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_U\) is singular (non-invertible) even if \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) is positive definite.

 

6.2 Stationarity of the VAR(\(p\)) process

In order the VAR(\(p\)) to define a stationary process (or even a stochastic process), the coefficient matrices \(\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\) must satisfy suitable conditions.

Stationarity (stability) condition of the VAR(\(p\)) process. We show that companion form based model equation defines a stationary process, if all of the eigenvalues of the matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) are smaller than one in absolute value.

This condition is equivalent to the determinant condition \[\begin{equation*} \det (\boldsymbol{I}_{Kp}-\mathbf{A} z)\neq 0,\ \ \left\vert z\right\vert \leq 1 , \quad |z|<1 \quad (z\in \mathbb{C}), \end{equation*}\] where \(|z|\) is the absolute value (or the modulus) of the complex number \(z\).

  • If \(z\in\mathbb{C}\), that is a complex number, i.e., \(z=x+iy\) (\(i^{2}=-1\)), then \(|z|=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\) is the modulus of \(z\).

Notice that when the process \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\) (written in terms of the companion form presentation) is (strictly) stationary, then so is the process \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\) as \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\) is a linear transformation of \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\).

 

  • To illustrate the stationarity assumption further, consider the VAR in a companion form when \(t\geq 1\) so that the process \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\) is well defined provided we specify the initial value \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}\). By iteration, we obtain \[\begin{align*} \boldsymbol{Y}_1 &= \boldsymbol{\ubar{\nu}} + \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{Y}_0 + \boldsymbol{U_1}, \\ \boldsymbol{Y}_2 &= \boldsymbol{\ubar{\nu}} + \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{Y}_1 + \boldsymbol{U}_2 = (\boldsymbol{I}_K + \mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{\ubar{\nu}} + \mathbf{A}^2 \boldsymbol{Y}_0 + \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{U}_1 + \boldsymbol{U}_2 \\ \quad\vdots \\ \boldsymbol{Y}_t & = \boldsymbol{\ubar{\mu}} + \mathbf{A}^t \boldsymbol{Y}_0 + \sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\mathbf{A}^j\boldsymbol{U}_{t-j}, \end{align*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{\ubar{\mu}} = [(\sum_{j=0}^{t-1} \mathbf{A}^j) \boldsymbol{\ubar{\nu}} \quad (\sum_{j=0}^{t-1} \mathbf{A}^j) \boldsymbol{\ubar{\nu}} \quad \cdots]\) (that is multiple copies of the same vector-valued element). In order to define a “suitable” initial value we note that when stationarity condition holds, then \(\mathbf{A}^j\rightarrow \boldsymbol{0}\) geometrically, as \(j\rightarrow \infty\), implying that \(\sum_{j=0}^\infty|\mathbf{A}^j|^{2}<\infty\) (see Appendix A).

  • We can hence define the (companion form-based) linear process (cf. the univariate case, univariate linear process) \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{Y}_t - \boldsymbol{\ubar{\mu}} = \mathbf{A}^t\sum_{j=0}^\infty \mathbf{A}^j \boldsymbol{U}_{-j} + \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \mathbf{A}^j \boldsymbol{U}_{t-j}, \quad t\geq 1. \end{equation*}\] It follows that \(\boldsymbol{Y}_t,t\geq 1\) is stationary. It is also clear that a process defined in this way yields a solution to the VAR(\(p\)) equation can be extended so that, under stability condition of the companion form, the VAR(\(p\)) process defines the stationary linear process \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{Y}_t - \boldsymbol{\ubar{\mu}} =\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \mathbf{A}^j \boldsymbol{U}_{t-j}. \end{equation*}\] Define the matrix \(\boldsymbol{J} = [\boldsymbol{I}_{K} \,\, \boldsymbol{0} \cdots \boldsymbol{0}] \quad (K \times Kp)\). We have \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{J} \boldsymbol{Y}_t \quad \text{and} \quad \boldsymbol{U}_t = \boldsymbol{J} ^\prime \boldsymbol{u}_t, \end{equation*}\] so that we get the VMA(\(\infty\)) presentation of the VAR(\(p\)) process \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_{t} - \boldsymbol{\mu} = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j} \boldsymbol{u}_{t-j}, \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_j = \boldsymbol{J} \mathbf{A}^j \boldsymbol{J}^\prime\) and hence \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{0} = \boldsymbol{I}_K\). Given that \(\mathbf{A}^j \rightarrow \boldsymbol{0}\) geometrically, as \(j\rightarrow \infty\), the matrices \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_j\) have the same property, especially \(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\Vert \boldsymbol{\Psi}_j \right\Vert^2 < \infty\).

If we consider \(\boldsymbol{y}_t\) over periods \(t\geq 1\) alone and allow nonstationary initial values (\(\boldsymbol{y}_{-p+1}, \ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\)), then \(\{\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\ t\geq 1\}\) is nonstationary. The above derivations show that the impact of the initial values disappears when \(t\) increases, so that eventually the process becomes stationary. In this type of setting, it is customary to say that the process is asymptotically stationary and \(\det (\boldsymbol{I}_{Kp}-\mathbf{A}z)\neq 0, \, \left\vert z\right\vert \leq 1 , \, |z|<1 \, (z\in \mathbb{C})\) is often called the stability condition.

 

Another Formulation for the stationarity (stability) condition. Using the lag operator \(\mathsf{B}\), we can rewrite the VAR(\(p\)) process simply as \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{\nu} + \boldsymbol{u}_t, \quad \boldsymbol{u}_{t}\sim \mathsf{iid}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u), \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}) = \boldsymbol{I}_K - \boldsymbol{A}_1 \mathsf{B} -\cdots - \boldsymbol{A}_p \mathsf{B}^{p}\). It can be shown that the above stationarity (stability) condition is equivalent to the condition \[\begin{equation*} \det \boldsymbol{A}(z)\neq 0, \quad |z|\leq 1 \end{equation*}\] Here \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)\) is a polynomial matrix, a matrix whose elements are polynomials, where the lag operator \(\mathsf{B}\) is the argument. Alternatively, \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)\) can be interpreted as a matrix polynomial, whose coefficients are matrices.

 

6.3 Linear process

By multiplying the VAR(\(p\)) process from the left by the inverse matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})^{-1}\), the above solution must be in short \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{u}_t, \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathsf{B}) = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_j \mathsf{B}^j = \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})^{-1}\).

  • Recall the result \(\boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})^{-1} = [1/\det \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}))] \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})^\ast\) where \(\boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})^\ast\) is a finite order matrix polynomial (see Appendix A).

(Multiple-equation and causal) linear process. Linear process for \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}=(y_{1t},...,y_{Kt})\) is defined by \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_j \boldsymbol{u}_{t-j}, \quad \boldsymbol{u}_t \sim \mathsf{iid}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u), \quad \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_j^2<\infty. \end{equation*}\]

  • The norm \(\left\Vert \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}\right\Vert\) can be defined by stacking the columns of \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}\) into a single \((K^{2}\times 1)\) vector and applying the standard Euclidean vector norm in \(\mathbb{R}^{K^2}\) (See Appendix A)

  • As introduced in the previous section, a (stationary) VAR(\(p\)) process is an example case of the above linear process.

The (strict) stationarity of the linear process \(\{\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\}\) follows from the strict stationarity of \(\{\boldsymbol{u} _{t}\}\), as in the univariate case. Weak stationarity is seen by computing \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}) = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j} \boldsymbol{u}_{t-j}\right) = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t-j})= \boldsymbol{\mu}, \end{equation*}\] and by defining a demeaned process \(\boldsymbol{z}_t = \boldsymbol{y}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}\), we get \[\begin{eqnarray*} \mathsf{Cov}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_t,\boldsymbol{y}_{t+h}\right) &=& \mathsf{Cov}\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{t} \boldsymbol{z}_{t+h}^{\prime} \right) \\ &=& \mathsf{E}\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{t} \boldsymbol{z}_{t+h}^{\prime }\right) \\ &=& \mathsf{E}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i} \boldsymbol{u}_{t-i} \boldsymbol{u}_{t+h-j}^\prime \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}^\prime\right) \\ &=&\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t-i} \boldsymbol{u}_{t+h-j}^\prime) \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}^\prime \\ &=&\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j+h}^\prime.\\ \end{eqnarray*}\] Here, we have made use of the linearity of expectation, the fact that the innovation series \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\) is independent implying that \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t-s} \boldsymbol{u}_{t+h-j}^\prime) = \boldsymbol{0}\) for all \(j\neq h+s\), and \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t} \boldsymbol{u}_{t}^\prime) = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\).

  • By the properties of matrix norm, \(\left\Vert \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}^\prime\right\Vert\leq\left\Vert \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \right\Vert\left\Vert \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}\right\Vert^{2}\) so that by employing the triangular inequality we can observe that it is finite (see Appendix A). It then follows that \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\) is weakly stationary.

  • As an additional remark, the above derivations on \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{y}_{t})\) and \(\mathsf{Cov}(\boldsymbol{y}_{t},\boldsymbol{y}_{t+h})\) also hold, when \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\) is just a serially uncorrelated series (i.e., \(\mathsf{Cov}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t},\boldsymbol{u}_{t+h})=\boldsymbol{0}\) for all \(h\neq 0\)) so that weak stationarity holds in this case too.

 

Covariance function of VAR(\(p\)) process. Given that a stationary VAR(\(p\)) process has the linear process representation, its mean (see above) and covariance function can be obtained by steps similar to those above and as a special case. We get \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h} = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty } \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j+h} = \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{-h}^\prime \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}\) are as obtained also above and more specifically to the selected VAR(\(p\)).

  • Because \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{0}\) geometrically, when \(j\rightarrow \infty\), the covariance function \(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{h}\) dampens geometrically as \(h\) tends to \(\infty\). The speed of dampening is slower the closer the absolute value of the largest eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{A}\) is to unity.

  • Note that in the case \(K=p=1\), the matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is an autoregressive coefficient and \(\mathbf{A}^l\), the \(l\):th autocorrelation coefficient).

 

6.4 VAR(\(p\)) and impulse response function

In the context of VMA(\(\infty\)) presentation, we stated that \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_j = \boldsymbol{J} \mathbf{A}^j \boldsymbol{J}^\prime\). An alternative way to express the matrices \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_j\) is obtained from the identity \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}) = \boldsymbol{I}_K\), that is, \[\begin{align*} \boldsymbol{I}_{K} &= (\boldsymbol{\Psi}_0 + \boldsymbol{\Psi}_1\mathsf{B} + \boldsymbol{\Psi}_2\mathsf{B}^2 + \cdots) \left(\boldsymbol{I}_K - \boldsymbol{A}_1\mathsf{B} -\cdots-\boldsymbol{A}_p\mathsf{B}^p\right) \\ &=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_0+(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_0 \boldsymbol{A}_1)\mathsf{B}+(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_2-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1 \boldsymbol{A}_1-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_0\boldsymbol{A}_2)\mathsf{B}^2+\cdots \text{ .} \end{align*}\] It follows that \[\begin{align*} \boldsymbol{I}_K &=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_0 \\ \boldsymbol{0} &=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_0 \boldsymbol{A}_1 \\ \boldsymbol{0} &=\boldsymbol{\Psi}_2-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1 \boldsymbol{A}_1-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_0 \boldsymbol{A}_2 \\ &\quad\vdots \end{align*}\] so that we can compute \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_1\) recursively by using equations \[\begin{align} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_0 &=\boldsymbol{I}_K \\ \boldsymbol{\Psi}_j &=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j-i}\boldsymbol{A}_i , \quad j=1,2,..., \end{align}\] where \(\boldsymbol{A}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}\), for \(i>p\).

As noted earlier, a VAR(\(p\)) model can be seen as a system of several linear regressions, where regressors are the lags of the response variables. Hence, compared with the usual linear regression, the regressors are selected in a particular way.

  • The unobserved error term is largely similar to the error of a conventional regression model. As in a regression, the coefficient matrices \(\boldsymbol{A}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\) can be interpreted to describe how lagged variables affect the response variables. However, the values of individual elements in these matrices can seldom be clearly interpreted in isolation.

Usually, instead of focusing on individual coefficients, we are interested in dynamic effects.

  • Specifically, how does a change in one of the variables, say \(y_{1t}\), affect the future paths of all components of \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\)?

It is difficult to describe this type of effect by looking at individual elements in the matrices \(\boldsymbol{A}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\).

  • This can bee seen already in the case \(K=2\). Draw a figure where the impacts of \(y_{1t}\) and \(y_{2t}\) on future values \(y_{1,t+j}\) and \(y_{2,t+j}\) are marked by arrows. The figure will be rather messy even if there are only two variables!

Another interpretation is based on the idea that the components of the error term \(\boldsymbol{u}_t\) are regarded as impulses or shocks that drive the variables.

  • In this case, we are interested in seeing how a unit-sized impulse affects (and spreads to) the components of the process and their future values. We will return to this question in more detail in the Macroeconometrics course!

However, from the recursion of \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_j\), we can quite easily see that elements of the matrices \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}\) describe how an impulse at time \(t_0\) affects the process \(\boldsymbol{y}_t\) at time \(t_{0}+j\). For this reason, \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{j}\) are at times called impulse responses.

  • More specifically, these are often referred to as forecast error impulse responses. As mentioned, in macroeconomic applications, we typically need to impose additional (identification) restrictions on the VAR model, leading to a structural VAR (SVAR) model. These models will be considered in more detail in the Macroeconometrics course.

 

6.5 Extra: VARMA(\(p,q\)) process

As we have seen earlier in this material, the univariate AR(\(p\)) process could be generalized into an ARMA(\(p,q\)) process by adding the linear combinations of \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t-1},\ldots, \boldsymbol{u}_{t-q}\) into the AR(\(p\)) model equation. The corresponding multivariate generalization, VARMA(\(p,q\)) process, is \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_{t} = \boldsymbol{\nu} + \boldsymbol{A}_{1} \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1} + \cdots + \boldsymbol{A}_{p} \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{u}_{t} - \boldsymbol{M}_{1} \boldsymbol{u}_{t-1} -\cdots - \boldsymbol{M}_{q} \boldsymbol{u}_{t-q}, \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\sim\mathsf{iid}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \right)\) and \(\boldsymbol{M}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{M}_q\) are coefficient matrices.

  • Other authors (like Lütkepohl) define the VARMA process using plus signs: \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_{t} = \boldsymbol{\nu} + \boldsymbol{A}_{1} \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1} + \cdots + \boldsymbol{A}_{p} \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{u}_{t} + \boldsymbol{M}_{1} \boldsymbol{u}_{t-1} + \cdots + \boldsymbol{M}_{q} \boldsymbol{u}_{t-q}. \end{equation*}\]

As in the univariate case, we can note that a sufficient condition for the stationarity of a VARMA(\(p,q\)) process is as for the VAR(\(p\)) process. Using a lag operator equation, the VARMA process can be written as \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})\boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{M}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{u}_t \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}) = \boldsymbol{I}_K - \boldsymbol{A}_1\mathsf(B)-\cdots-\boldsymbol{A}_p\mathsf{B}^p\) and \(\boldsymbol{M}(\mathsf{B}) = \boldsymbol{I}_K - \boldsymbol{M}_1\mathsf{B}-\cdots- \boldsymbol{M}_p\mathsf{B}^q\). When the stationarity condition holds, this equation can be multiplied from the left by the inverse matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})^{-1}\) to get the well-defined linear process \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathsf{B})\boldsymbol{u}_t, \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\mathsf{B}) = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_j\mathsf{B}^j = \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})^{-1} \boldsymbol{M} (\mathsf{B})\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_0 = \boldsymbol{I}_K\). As with the VAR(\(p\)) process, \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_j\rightarrow \boldsymbol{0}\) geometrically as \(j\rightarrow\infty\) and as such the covariance function of the VARMA(\(p,q\)) process dampens geometrically to zero as the lag grows infinitely.

The theory and application of VARMA(\(p,q\)) processes is significantly more challenging than VAR(\(p\)) processes.

  • This is largely due to the properties of the polynomial matrices, which makes the structural theory and model selection more complicated than with VAR(\(p\)) processes.

  • This can be seen especially with the uniqueness and identification conditions, which in the univariate case \(K=1\) requires that (now) the polynomials \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)\) and \(\boldsymbol{M}(z)\) do not have common factors and at least one of the higher order coefficients is non-zero.

The next example (Extra below) demonstrates why the uniqueness becomes more complicated in the multivariate case \(K>1\).

 

Extra: Example of a bivariate VARMA(1,1) process

Let us consider as an example of VARMA processes. Specifically we take a look on a bivariate zero-mean (for simplicity) VARMA(1,1) process \[\begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & a\mathsf{B} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & m\mathsf{B} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{u}_t, \ \ \ a\neq m. \end{equation*}\] The first order polynomial matrices \(\boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})\) and \(\boldsymbol{M}(\mathsf{B})\) seem to fulfill a condition similar to the uniqueness condition of the univariate ARMA(1,1) process. By multiplying the model equation above from the left by the inverse matrix \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -a\mathsf{B} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}\] we get the linear presentation of the process \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & (m-a)\mathsf{B} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{u}_t \end{equation*}\] which is a vector MA(1) or VMA(1) process (correspondingly we could talk about a VMA(\(q\)) process where \(q=\infty\) is also possible). By multiplying model equation from the left by the inverse matrix \(\boldsymbol{M}(\mathsf{B}^{-1}\) we get correspondingly \[\begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & (a-m)\mathsf{B} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{u}_t \end{equation*}\] or a VAR(1) process. Hence a VARMA(1,1) process (as above) can be presented as a VAR(1) and a VMA(1) process. If in this kind of a situation we are trying to fit the data with a VARMA(1,1) model, the estimation procedure gets into trouble, as it finds three models that fit the data equally well. The problem gets resolved of course if there is prior knowledge on the structure of the process which enables to choose either a VAR(1) or VMA(1) model to be estimated. Usually however, this kind of prior knowledge is not at hand and in more complicated cases even recognizing the problem isn’t as easy as in this simple example.

 

A rather complete uniqueness theory has been developed for VARMA(\(p,q\)) processes on which model selection can be applied in practice using some model selection criteria.

  • A theory for estimating (Gaussian) maximum likelihood has also been developed. These questions will be, however, ignored wholly on this course.

  • The statistical methods based on VAR(\(p\)) processes which have been described can be justified as approximations, as when the invertibility condition \[\begin{equation*} \mathrm{det} (\boldsymbol{M}(z))\neq 0, \qquad |z|\leq 1, \end{equation*}\] holds, the VARMA(\(p,q\)) process can be presented as \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{M}(\mathsf{B}^{-1} \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B}) \boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{u}_t, \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{M}(\mathsf{B})^{-1} \boldsymbol{A}(\mathsf{B})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{\Pi}_j \mathsf{B}^j\) and the coefficient matrices \(\boldsymbol{\Pi}_j\) converge to zero geometrically as \(j\rightarrow\infty \quad (\boldsymbol{\Pi}_0 = \boldsymbol{I}_K)\).

VARMA(\(p,q\)) process can thus be approximated with a VAR(\(h\)) process as long a \(h\) is “sufficiently large”.
