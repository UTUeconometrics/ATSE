10  Granger and instantaneous causality

10.1 General idea of Granger causality

Consider two real-valued random variables \(x\) and \(z\). How should one define causality from the former to the latter variable? Without delving deeper to the philosophical implications, one can think that a necessary condition for causality is that the “cause” does not occur after the “consequence”. If \(x\) is a cause for \(z\), then one expects that \(x\) has predictive content for \(z\).

Let us formalize this intuition. Let \(x_{t}\) and \(z_{t}\) be real-valued stochastic processes and consider predicting the future values of \(z_{t}\) at time \(t\).

  • Let \(\mathcal{F}_{t}\) denote the information set (as already described in forecasting sections above) hypothetical variables set, which includes \(x_{t}\) and all other variables that are observed at time \(t\) and could be used to predict the future values \(z_{t+h},\ h\geq 1\).

  • The optimal (minimum mean square error) prediction of \(z_{t+h}\) given \(\mathcal{F}_{t}\) is denoted \(z_{t}(h|\mathcal{F}_{t})\).

  • Correspondingly, let \(z_{t}(h|\mathcal{F}_{t}\backslash \mathcal{F}_{t}^{x})\) be the optimal prediction of \(z_{t+h}\) when the history of \(x\), denoted by \(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}=\left\{ x_{s},s\leq t\right\}\), is removed from the information set.

Let \(\Sigma_{z}(h|\mathcal{F}_{t})\) and \(\Sigma_{z}(h|\mathcal{F}_{t}\backslash \mathcal{F}_{t}^{x})\) be the corresponding mean square errors (MSE). We say that \(x_{t}\) Granger causes \(z_{t}\) if \[\begin{equation*} \Sigma_{z}(h|\mathcal{F}_{t})<\Sigma _{z}(h|\mathcal{F}_{t}\backslash \mathcal{F}_{t}^{x}) \end{equation*}\] for at least one \(t\) and \(h\in\{1,2,...\}\). That is, under Granger causality, we can obtain more accurate predictions for \(z_{t+h}\) (for some \(h>0\)) by exploiting information on the process \(x_{t}\).

If \(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\) and \(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\) are vector-valued processes, the mean square errors become matrices. In this case, we say that \(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\) Granger causes \(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\) if \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(h|\mathcal{F}_{t}\backslash\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\boldsymbol{x}})\neq \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(h|\mathcal{F}_{t}) \end{equation*}\] for at least for one \(h > 0\). The interpretation remains essentially similar: the history of \(\boldsymbol{x}_t\) improves the prediction of \(\boldsymbol{z}_t\).

 

10.2 Granger causality in VAR models

The information set \(\mathcal{F}_{t}\) applied in the general definition above is often infeasible in practice. In applied work, the information set is restricted to selected observed variables. Granger causality is most often studied in the context of VAR models, where the relevant information set consists of the components of the vector process and their lags.

Bivariate VAR model (\(K=2\)): Granger causality can be easily tested within a VAR model. Consider first, for simplicity, a bivariate VAR(\(p\)) process \[\begin{eqnarray*} \left[ \begin{array}{c} y_{1t} \\ y_{2t} \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \nu_{1} \\ \nu_{2} \end{array} \right] + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \left[ \begin{array}{cc} a_{11,j} & a_{12,j} \\ a_{21,j} & a_{22,j} \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} y_{1, t-j} \\ y_{2, t-j} \end{array} \right] + \left[ \begin{array}{c} u_{1t} \\ u_{2t} \end{array} \right]. \end{eqnarray*}\] If \[\begin{equation*} a_{12,j}=0, \, j=1,2, \ldots, p, \end{equation*}\] then the lags of \(y_{2t}\) do not help to predict \(y_{1t}\) and there is no Granger causality from \(y_{2t}\) to \(y_{1t}\) (that is, \(y_{2t}\) is not Granger-causal for \(y_{1t}\)).

  • That is, the restriction \(a_{12,1} = a_{12,2} = \cdots = a_{12,p} = 0\) implies the absence of such a Granger causality relationship.

When working with an estimated VAR model (for the empirical data), this hypothesis can be tested by the Wald or likelihood ratio (LR) test, following a \(\chi^2_p\)-distribution under the null hypothesis.

  • At times, such as in the vars package in R, the \(F\)-test statistic is used instead of the Wald test statistic, but the logic is the same.

 

Larger VAR models (\(K > 2\)) and indirect effects: When the VAR model contains more than two variables, Granger causality between two specific variables cannot always be tested by testing zero-restrictions in a simple pairwise manner. Consider the following three-variable VAR process: \[\begin{eqnarray*} \left[ \begin{array}{c} y_{1t} \\ y_{2t} \\ y_{3t} \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \nu_{1} \\ \nu_{2} \\ \nu_{3} \end{array} \right] + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \left[ \begin{array}{ccc} a_{11,j} & a_{12,j} & a_{13,j} \\ a_{21,j} & a_{22,j} & a_{23,j} \\ a_{31,j} & a_{32,j} & a_{33,j} \\ \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} y_{1, t-j} \\ y_{2, t-j} \\ y_{3, t-j} \end{array} \right] + \left[ \begin{array}{c} u_{1t} \\ u_{2t} \\ u_{3t} \end{array} \right]. \end{eqnarray*}\] If, for instance, \(a_{12,1}= \cdots = a_{12,p} = 0\), the lags of \(y_{2t}\) do not help forecasting \(y_{1t}\) one period ahead. However, because the lags of \(y_{2t}\) may affect \(y_{3t}\), which in turn affects \(y_{1t}\), the lags of \(y_{2t}\) may help forecasting \(y_{1,t+1}\), \(y_{1,t+2}\) etc. In other words, there may be Granger causality through indirect effects.

 

General condition. Generally, we can consider Granger causality from one group of variables to another. Consider a stationary VAR(\(p\)) process and partition \(\boldsymbol{y}_t = [\boldsymbol{y}_{1t}^{\prime} \quad \boldsymbol{y}_{2t}^{\prime}]^{\prime}\), where \(\boldsymbol{y}_{1t}\) is \(K_1 \times 1\) and \(\boldsymbol{y}_{2t}\) is \(K_2 \times 1\) , (\(K_1+K_2= K\)), and write the VAR(\(p\)) process \[\begin{eqnarray*} \left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{y}_{1t} \\ \boldsymbol{y}_{2t} \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{\nu}_1 \\ \boldsymbol{\nu}_2 \end{array} \right] + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \left[ \begin{array}{cc} \boldsymbol{A}_{11,j} & \boldsymbol{A}_{12,j} \\ \boldsymbol{A}_{21,j} & \boldsymbol{A}_{22,j} \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{y}_{1, t-j} \\ \boldsymbol{y}_{2, t-j} \end{array} \right] + \left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1t} \\ \boldsymbol{\zeta}_{2t} \end{array} \right]. \end{eqnarray*}\] Here \(\boldsymbol{A}_{\cdot \cdot,j}\) are matrices. It intuitively seems that Granger causality from \(\boldsymbol{y}_{2t}\) to \(\boldsymbol{y}_{1t}\) relates to whether \(\boldsymbol{A}_{12,j}\neq \boldsymbol{0}\) for some \(j\). In other words, variables in \(\boldsymbol{y}_{2t}\) does not Granger cause \(\boldsymbol{y}_{1t}\), if and only if \(\boldsymbol{A}_{12,j}=\boldsymbol{0}\) for all \(j=1,...,p\).

 

Extra: More details for Granger causality

For simplicity assume \(p=1\) and denote \(\boldsymbol{A}_{ij,1}=\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}\). The general version follows similar logic.

Assume \(\boldsymbol{A}_{12}=\boldsymbol{0}\). Then the equation above implies a stationary VAR(1) presentation for \(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}\): \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{z}_{t}=\boldsymbol{A}_{11}\boldsymbol{z}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}. \end{equation*}\] It follows that the conditional expectation depends only on the history of \(\boldsymbol{z}\): \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+h}|\boldsymbol{z}_{s},\ s\leq t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{11}\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+h-1}|\boldsymbol{z}_{s},\ s\leq t),\ \ h\geq 1. \end{equation*}\] On the other hand, when \(\boldsymbol{A}_{12}=\boldsymbol{0}\), the full model implies: \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+h}|\boldsymbol{y}_{s},\ s\leq t)=\boldsymbol{A}_{11}\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+h-1}|\boldsymbol{y}_{s},\ s\leq t),\ \ h\geq 1. \end{equation*}\] Comparing these two recursions (starting from \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}|\cdot) = \boldsymbol{A}_{11}\boldsymbol{z}_t\)), one observes by induction that the predictions are identical for all \(h\) and \(t\). Hence, there is no Granger causality.

Now, assume \(\boldsymbol{A}_{12}\neq \boldsymbol{0}\). As \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t+1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{y}_{s},s\leq t\), are independent, we have \(\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t+1}|\boldsymbol{y}_{s},\ s\leq t)=\boldsymbol{0}\) and the model yields: \[\begin{align*} \boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}-\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}|\boldsymbol{y}_{s},\ s\leq t) &= \boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{11}\boldsymbol{z}_{t}-\boldsymbol{A}_{12}\boldsymbol{x}_{t} \\ &= \boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t+1}. \end{align*}\] However, projecting only on the history of \(z\): \[\begin{align*} \boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}-\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}|\boldsymbol{z}_{s},\ s\leq t) &= \boldsymbol{z}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{11}\boldsymbol{z}_t-\boldsymbol{A}_{12}\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{x}_t|\boldsymbol{z}_t, \ s \leq t) \\ &= \boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t+1}+\boldsymbol{A}_{12}[\boldsymbol{x}_t-\mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{x}_t|\boldsymbol{z}_s, \ s\leq t)]. \end{align*}\] The last term is non-zero because \(\boldsymbol{A}_{12}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\) is not a function of the history of \(\boldsymbol{z}\) alone. Thus, the mean square error (covariance matrix of the prediction error) differs between the two information sets. It follows that \(\boldsymbol{x}\) Granger causes \(\boldsymbol{z}\). \(\square\)

 

The “no Granger causality” hypothesis corresponds to \(K_1 K_2 p\) zero restrictions: \[\begin{equation*} H_0: \boldsymbol{A}_{12,1} = \boldsymbol{A}_{12,2} = \cdots = \boldsymbol{A}_{12,p} = \boldsymbol{0}. \end{equation*}\] This hypothesis can be tested using Wald or Likelihood Ratio (LR) tests.

  • Under the null hypothesis, these test statistics are asymptotically \(\chi^2_{K_1 K_2 p}\)-distributed.

  • Large values of the test statistic lead to rejection of the null hypothesis (i.e., finding evidence of Granger causality).

  • As mentioned, the \(F\)-test is also commonly used in finite samples (e.g., in R/RStudio).

 

Example: Three-variable process. Consider the VAR(1) process: \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \left[ \begin{array}{c} y_{1t} \\ y_{2t} \\ y_{3t} \end{array} \right] = \left[ \begin{array}{c} 0 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{array} \right] + \left[ \begin{array}{ccc} 0.5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0.1 & 0.1 & 0.3 \\ 0 & 0.2 & 0.3 \end{array} \right] \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1} +\boldsymbol{u}_t. \end{equation*}\] Partitioning \(\boldsymbol{y}_t = [y_{1t} \quad \boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{2t}]^{\prime}\) so that \(\boldsymbol{y}_{2t} = [y_{2t} \quad y_{3t}]^{\prime}\).

  • Here, the sub-matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}_{12}\) corresponds to the top-right block \([0 \quad 0]\). Since this is zero, \(\boldsymbol{y}_{2t}\) (variables \(y_{2t}\) and \(y_{3t}\)) does not Granger cause \(y_{1t}\).

  • On the other hand, looking at the coefficients affecting \(y_{2t}\) (the second row), the coefficient for \(y_{1,t-1}\) is \(0.1 \neq 0\). Thus, \(y_{1t}\) Granger causes \(\boldsymbol{y}_{2t}\).

 

Granger causality when involving \(I(1)\) variables. Standard Granger causality tests (such as using F or Wald tests in a standard VAR), as above, rely on the assumption that variables are stationary (\(I(0)\)).

  • If you apply standard tests to non-stationary \(I(1)\) variables, the test statistics will not follow standard asymptotic distributions (like \(\chi^2\) or \(F\) distributions).

  • This often leads to “spurious causality”—finding relationships where none exist.

Different scenarios:

  • Scenario A: Variables are \(I(1)\) and cointegrated. If variables share a long-run equilibrium (cointegration), you must use a Vector Error Correction Model (VECM). Cointegration and VECM will be introduced in the next section.

  • Scenario B: Variables are \(I(1)\) and not cointegrated. If variables are non-stationary and have no long-run relationship, you must difference them. Using differenced time series, the limitation is that tests test short-run causality only. Long-run information is removed by differencing (but this is acceptable since no long-run relationship exists).

  • Scenario C: The robust and preferred solution is the Toda-Yamamoto (TY) procedure, which is valid regardless of whether the variables are \(I(0)\), \(I(1)\), or cointegrated. It avoids the potential biases associated with pre-testing for cointegration. The core idea is to estimate a VAR in levels (not differences) but add extra lags to “fix” the asymptotic distribution of the Wald test statistics. Steps:

    1. Select lag length (\(p\)): Determine the optimal lag length \(p\) for the VAR using, for example, information criteria (AIC, SC, or HQ).

    2. Determine integration order (\(d_{max}\)): Identify the maximum order of integration for the variables in the system (usually \(d_{max}=1\) for (economic) time series).

    3. Estimate augmented VAR: Estimate a VAR in levels with a total lag length of \(p + d_{max}\).

    4. Perform Wald Test: Test the Granger causality restrictions only on the first \(p\) lags. The coefficients for the extra \(d_{max}\) lags are ignored; they function essentially as nuisance parameters to ensure the test statistic follows a standard \(\chi^2\) distribution. Therefore, the test regression for a bivariate system where we test if \(\boldsymbol{x}\) Granger-causes \(\boldsymbol{y}\):

    \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_t = \boldsymbol{\nu} + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \boldsymbol{A}_i \boldsymbol{y}_{t-i} + \underbrace{\sum_{j=p+1}^{p+d_{max}} \boldsymbol{A}_j \boldsymbol{y}_{t-j}}_{\text{ignored lags}} + \sum_{i=1}^{p} \boldsymbol{B}_i \boldsymbol{x}_{t-i} + \underbrace{\sum_{j=p+1}^{p+d_{max}} \boldsymbol{B}_j \boldsymbol{x}_{t-j}}_{\text{ignored lags}} + u_t \end{equation*}\] and test the hypothesis \[\begin{equation*} H_0: \boldsymbol{B}_1 = \boldsymbol{B}_2 = \cdots = \boldsymbol{B}_p = \boldsymbol{0}. \end{equation*}\]

 

10.3 Instantaneous (contemporaneous) causality

The definition of Granger causality discussed above strictly concerns predictive causality: whether past values of a variable help predict future values of another. It does not address instantaneous (or contemporaneous) causality—that is, whether knowing the value of \(x_{t+1}\) helps to more accurately forecast \(z_{t+1}\) given the history \(\mathcal{F}_t\).

Formally, there exists instantaneous Granger causality between processes \(x_t\) and \(z_t\) if: \[\begin{equation*} \Sigma_z(1|\mathcal{F}_t) \neq \Sigma_z(1|\mathcal{F}_t \cup \{x_{t+1}\}). \end{equation*}\] Here, the variable set \(\mathcal{F}_t \cup \{x_{t+1}\}\) includes the future realization \(x_{t+1}\) in addition to the history \(\mathcal{F}_t\). In other words, adding the contemporaneous value of \(x\) to the information set improves the forecast of \(z\).

 

Symmetry and interpretation. A crucial property of instantaneous causality is symmetry. It can be shown that if \(x_t\) is instantaneously causal for \(z_t\), then \(z_t\) is also instantaneously causal for \(x_t\).

  • Consequently, this statistical definition does not determine the “direction” of causality.

  • The direction must be inferred from other sources, such as economic theory. For instance, if theory suggests causality runs only from \(x\) to \(z\), a significant instantaneous relationship can be interpreted in favor of that causal link.

 

Instantaneous causality in VAR models. In the context of VAR models, instantaneous causality is directly linked to the correlation of the error terms.

  • Bivariate case (\(K=2\)): In a bivariate VAR, there is no instantaneous causality between \(y_{1t}\) and \(y_{2t}\) if and only if the errors \(u_{1t}\) and \(u_{2t}\) are uncorrelated. If the errors are correlated, knowing the realization of \(y_{1t}\) (and thus \(u_{1t}\)) provides information about \(u_{2t}\), thereby improving the forecast of \(y_{2t}\).

  • General case (\(K > 2\)): In systems with more than two variables, the absence of instantaneous causality corresponds to the structure of the error covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\). There is no instantaneous causality between two groups of variables if \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) is block diagonal with respect to those groups.

    Example: Consider a VAR process for \(\boldsymbol{y}_t = (y_{1t}, y_{2t}, y_{3t})\) with the following error covariance matrix: \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u = \left[ \begin{array}{ccc} 2.0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1.5 & 0.4 \\ 0 & 0.4 & 1.0 \\ \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}\] Here, the covariance between the error of \(y_{1t}\) and the errors of the other variables is zero (the top row and left column off-diagonal elements are 0). However, the errors for \(y_{2t}\) and \(y_{3t}\) are correlated (\(0.4\)). Conclusion: There is no instantaneous causality between \(y_{1t}\) and the group \(\boldsymbol{y}_{2t} = (y_{2t}, y_{3t})\).

Testing: Instantaneous causality can be tested statistically using a Wald-type test for zero restrictions on the corresponding off-diagonal elements of the error covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\).
