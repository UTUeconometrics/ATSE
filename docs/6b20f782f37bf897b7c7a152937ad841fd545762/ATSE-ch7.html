7  VAR(\(p\)): Estimation and hypothesis testing

7.1 Parameter estimation

Consider an \(K\)-dimensional observed time series that can be described by the VAR(\(p\)) model \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_{t} = \boldsymbol{\nu} + \boldsymbol{A}_{1} \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1} + \cdots + \boldsymbol{A}_{p} \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p} + \boldsymbol{u}_{t}, \quad t=1,\ldots,T, \end{equation*}\] where the initial values \(\boldsymbol{y}_{-p+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\) are also observed and the coefficient matrices \(\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\) meet the stationarity/stability condition of the VAR(\(p\)) process.

In order to apply maximum likelihood estimation, assume that the error term is normally distributed \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{u}_{t}\sim \mathsf{nid}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u), \end{equation*}\] where the covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) is positive definite. The asymptotic results below will hold also under the more general assumption \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\sim \mathsf{iid}(\boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)\).

 

Likelihood function. Define the notation \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{x}_{t}= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}^{\prime } & \cdots & \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p}^{\prime} \end{bmatrix}^{\prime} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}^{\prime} \end{bmatrix}^{\prime} \qquad \qquad ((Kp+1)\times 1) \end{equation*}\] and \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Pi} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\pi}_1^\prime \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{\pi}_K^\prime \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\nu} : & \boldsymbol{A}_1: & \dots & :\boldsymbol{A}_p \end{bmatrix}. \qquad \qquad (K \times (Kp+1)) \end{equation*}\]

Write the VAR(\(p\)) model as \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_{t} = \boldsymbol{\Pi} \boldsymbol{x}_{t} + \boldsymbol{u}_{t}, \quad t=1,\ldots,T. \end{equation*}\] This constitutes the equations (\(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^\prime \boldsymbol{x}_{t} =\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}\)) \[\begin{equation*} y_{kt} = \boldsymbol{x}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi}_{k} + u_{kt},\qquad k=1, \ldots ,K, \quad t=1,\ldots,T. \end{equation*}\] Hence we can rewrite the model as \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_{t} = \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi} + \boldsymbol{u}_{t}, \qquad t=1,\ldots,T, \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{\pi} =\left[\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}^\prime \ \cdots \ \boldsymbol{\pi}_{K}^\prime\right]^{\prime }\,\, (K(Kp+1)\times 1)\), and \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\prime } = \mathsf{diag}[\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\prime }\ \cdots \ \boldsymbol{x}_{t}^\prime] = \boldsymbol{I}_{K}\otimes \boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\prime}, \qquad \qquad (K \times K(Kp+1)) \end{equation*}\] where \(\otimes\) denotes Kronecker product (see Appendix A).

 

(Conditional) likelihood function. To derive the likelihood function, we will follow the same principles as with univariate AR and ARMA models. Denote \[\begin{equation*} \ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{t} = \left[\boldsymbol{y}_{-p+1}^\prime \quad \cdots \quad \boldsymbol{y}_{0}^\prime \quad \boldsymbol{y}_{1}^\prime \quad \cdots \quad \boldsymbol{y}_{t}^\prime \right]^\prime, \quad t=0,1,\ldots,T. \end{equation*}\] Denote the joint density of \(\mathbf{\underline{Y}}_{t}\) by \(f_{\mathbf{\underline{Y}}_{t}}\). Then the joint density of the whole sample can be derived by using the formula of the conditional density (as in the univariate case!): \[\begin{equation*} f_{\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{T}}=f_{\boldsymbol{y}_{T}|\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{T-1}}\cdot f_{\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{T-1}}=f_{\boldsymbol{y}_{T}|\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{T-1}}\cdot f_{\boldsymbol{y}_{T-1}|\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{T-2}} \cdot f_{\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{T-2}}=\cdots =\prod_{t=1}^{T}f_{\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{t-1}}\cdot f_{\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{0}} \end{equation*}\] The linear process representation of the VAR(\(p\)) process implies that \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t-j}\) and \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\), \(j\geq 1\), are independent and in particular that \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}\) and \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\) are independent.

  • This is the result we have already used in univariate models and now we extend it to this multiple-equation case.

This and the VAR(\(p\)) model equation together imply that the conditional density of \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\) given \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}\) depends only on the first \(p\) subvectors \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{t-p}\), or just \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}\). Therefore, we get the distribution result \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{t-1} \quad \mathrm{or} \quad \boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}\sim \mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u). \end{equation*}\]

  • Here one can use the following general result that is related to the multivariate normal distribution. If (for some general matrices with suitable dimensions) \(\boldsymbol{Y} = \boldsymbol{X} + \boldsymbol{Z}\), where \(\boldsymbol{X}\) and \(\boldsymbol{Z}\) are independent and \(\boldsymbol{Z} \sim \mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), then \(\boldsymbol{Y}|(\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})\sim \mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\).

Given the formula of the density function of a multivariate normal, we have \[\begin{equation*} f_{\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\ubar{\mathbf{Y}}_{t-1}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}|\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}) = \left(2\pi\right)^{-1/2} \det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)^{-1/2} \exp \left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left( \boldsymbol{y}_{t}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi} \right)^{\prime} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u \left(\boldsymbol{y}_{t} - \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi}\right)\right\}. \end{equation*}\]

In order to obtain the density (\(f_{\mathbf{\ubar{Y}}_{T}}\)) of the whole sample (\(\mathbf{\ubar{Y}}_{T}\)) we must still find the distribution of the initial value vector \(\mathbf{\ubar{Y}}_{0}\). In what follows, we interpret \(\boldsymbol{\ubar{Y}}_{0}\) as fixed, or equivalently we condition the underlying joint density on its value. This results in the so called conditional likelihood function, which treats the product \[\begin{equation*} f_{\boldsymbol{y}_{1}|\mathbf{\ubar{Y}}_{0}}\cdots f_{\boldsymbol{y}_{T}|\mathbf{\ubar{Y}}_{T-1}} \end{equation*}\] as a function of the parameters. Taking the logarithmic terms we obtain the so called log-likelihood function (we ignore the constant part that is independent of the parameter) \[\begin{equation*} l(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) = -\frac{T}{2}\log\left(\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi} \right)^\prime \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \left( \boldsymbol{y}_{t}- \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi} \right). \end{equation*}\] It is clear that in large samples the impact of the initial values \(\boldsymbol{y}_{-p+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\) is negligible and that the initial values have no impact on the asymptotic properties of the likelihood function.

  • Unless stated otherwise, hereafter likelihood function refers to this conditional likelihood function.

 

Maximazing likelihood function. Consider maximizing the likelihood function with respect to the parameters of the model. Take the partial derivative with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) of the (conditional) log-likelihood function (see Appendix B) \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\pi}} l(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{X}_{t} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{t} - \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi} \right). \end{equation*}\] By the definition of the matrix \(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\) and using the properties of the Kronecker product (see Appendix A) we obtain \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{X}_{t} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} = \left(\boldsymbol{I}_{K}\otimes \boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \otimes 1 \right) = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \otimes \boldsymbol{x}_{t} = \left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \otimes \boldsymbol{I}_{Kp+1}\right) \left(\boldsymbol{I}_K \otimes \boldsymbol{x}_{t}\right), \end{equation*}\] where \(\left(\boldsymbol{I}_K \otimes \boldsymbol{x}_{t} \right) = \boldsymbol{X}_{t}\). Hence, we can write \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\pi}} l(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) = \left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \otimes \boldsymbol{I}_{Kp+1} \right) \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{X}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{t} - \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi} \right). \end{equation*}\] Setting \(\partial l(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)/\partial \boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{0}\), we can subtract the (non-singular) factor \(\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \otimes \boldsymbol{I}_{Kp+1}\right)\) so that the likelihood equation concerning the parameter \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) is \[\begin{equation*} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{X}_{t} \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi} =\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{X}_{t} \boldsymbol{y}_{t}. \end{equation*}\] This equation yields the unique solution \[\begin{equation*} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} = \left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{X}_{t} \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime\right)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{X}_{t} \boldsymbol{y}_{t}, \end{equation*}\] which is the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator of the coefficient parameters of the VAR model.

For further illustration, write the underlying system of equations in matrix form as \[\begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{y}_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{y}_{T} \end{array} \right] =\left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{X}_{1}^\prime \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{X}_{T}^\prime \end{array} \right] +\left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{u}_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{u}_{T} \end{array} \right], \end{equation*}\] or in short \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\ubar{y}} = \boldsymbol{\ubar{X}} \boldsymbol{\pi} + \boldsymbol{\ubar{u}}. \end{equation*}\] Using straightforward matrix calculus one see that the OLS estimator is obtained by the usual OLS formula \[\begin{equation*} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} = \left(\boldsymbol{\ubar{X}}^\prime \boldsymbol{\ubar{X}} \right)^{-1} \boldsymbol{\ubar{X}}^\prime \boldsymbol{\ubar{y}}. \end{equation*}\]

  • Note, however, that \(\mathsf{Cov}(\boldsymbol{u}) = \boldsymbol{I}_{T} \otimes \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\), which differs from the standard assumption of a linear model.

  • The reason why MLE still yields the OLS estimator has to do with the special structure of the regressor matrix \(\boldsymbol{\ubar{X}}\).

  • Later we will see that the result does not apply when the structure of \(\boldsymbol{\ubar{X}}\) changes. By direct calculation one can see that the subvectors \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{K}\) of \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) are the standard OLS estimators based on the individual equations.

It turns out that the maximum likelihood estimator for the covariance matrix is \[\begin{equation*} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}-\boldsymbol{X}_t^{\prime} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right) \left(\boldsymbol{y}_t-\boldsymbol{X}_t^\prime\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right)^\prime. \end{equation*}\] Details on how to obtain it are provided below as Extra information.

 

Extra: Covariance matrix estimation

Consider next the maximum likelihood estimation of the covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\). It is handy to first derive MLE for the inverse matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \equiv \boldsymbol{\Phi}\) and then (by the invariance property of the MLE) obtain the MLE for \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) through the inverse \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\). Applying the parameter matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\) and the short-hand notation \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}) = \boldsymbol{y}_{t}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi}\), we can write the log-likelihood as \[\begin{equation*} l(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) = \frac{T}{2}\log\left(\det(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\right) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{u}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi})^\prime \boldsymbol{\Phi} \boldsymbol{u}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}). \end{equation*}\]

  • Note that we have here simplified the derivation by (erroneously) not taking the symmetry of \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\) into account. As noted in Appendix A, when we account for the symmetry correctly, the derivative becomes more complicated, while still the same likelihood equation is obtained for \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\) and hence the same MLE. Intuitively, this is plausible, because the maximization results in a symmetric (and positive definite) solution, even if the symmetry condition is ignored.

Applying the derivation rules of Appendix A, we obtain \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\Phi}} \boldsymbol{u}_t(\boldsymbol{\pi})^\prime \boldsymbol{\Phi} \boldsymbol{u}_t(\boldsymbol{\pi}) = \boldsymbol{u}_t(\boldsymbol{\pi}) \boldsymbol{u}_t(\boldsymbol{\pi})^\prime \quad \mathrm{and} \quad \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\Phi}}\log\left(\det(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\right) = \boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \end{equation*}\] so that \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\Phi}}l(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) = \frac{T}{2} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{u}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}) \boldsymbol{u}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi})^\prime \end{equation*}\] Setting the right hand side to zero and replacing \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) in \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi})\) by the above derived ML solution \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) we obtain the solution \[\begin{equation*} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}-\boldsymbol{X}_t^{\prime} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right) \left(\boldsymbol{y}_t-\boldsymbol{X}_t^\prime\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right)^\prime. \end{equation*}\] Given that \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) minimizes the log-likelihood function \(l(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)\), we can conclude on the basis of the invariance property of MLE that \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) and \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\) are (conditional) ML estimators for \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\).

 

Asympotic properties of maximum likelihood estimators. Our starting point is (as is usual in the MLE theory) that standard limit theorems (the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem) apply under the considered VAR(\(p\)) model. To simplify notation assume the initial values \(\boldsymbol{y}_{-p+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\) are stationary.

To obtain the asymptotic distribution of \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) compute \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\pi} \partial \boldsymbol{\pi}^\prime}l(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) = -\left( \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \otimes \boldsymbol{I}_{Kp+1}\right) \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{X}_{t} \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{\prime } = -\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_t \boldsymbol{x}_t^\prime, \end{equation*}\] where the latter equation is implied by \(\boldsymbol{X}_t = \boldsymbol{I}_K \otimes \boldsymbol{x}_t\) and the properties of the Kronecker product. As defined above, \(\boldsymbol{x}_{t} = [1 \quad \boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}^\prime]^\prime\), the Fisher information matrix for the parameter \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) is \[\begin{equation*} \mathcal{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\boldsymbol{\pi} \boldsymbol{\pi}} (\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) \equiv-\mathsf{E}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\pi} \partial \boldsymbol{\pi}^\prime} l(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)\right] = T \left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \otimes \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{x}} \right), \end{equation*}\] where \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Gamma }_{\boldsymbol{x}} = \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^\prime) = \left[ \begin{array}{cc} 1 & \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}^\prime) \\ \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}^\prime) & \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}^\prime) \end{array} \right]. \end{equation*}\]

Next note that (see also Appendix A) \[\begin{align*} \mathsf{E}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\pi} \partial\sigma_{ij}} l(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u )\right] & = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathsf{E}\left[\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\left( \frac{\partial}{\partial \sigma_{ij}} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \right)(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}-\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi})\right] \\ & = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathsf{E}\left[\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma_{ij}} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \right)\right] \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}) \\ & = \boldsymbol{0}. \end{align*}\] Here the second equation follows from \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t} = \boldsymbol{y}_{t} - \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\pi}\) and the fact that \(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\) is independent of \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}, \boldsymbol{y}_{t-2},\ldots\) and hence of \(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\) (see the definition of \(\boldsymbol{X}_{t}\)).

The above derivations imply that the Fisher information matrix for \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) is block diagonal, i.e., the parameters \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) are orthogonal.

  • The general maximum likelihood theory then implies that the estimators \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) and \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\) are asymptotically independent and the asymptotic distribution of \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) is as in a situation where \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) is known.

In other words, \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) is asymptotically normal with expectation \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) and the covariance matrix \(\mathcal{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\boldsymbol{\pi} \boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{\pi}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)^{-1}\). As \(\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \otimes \boldsymbol{\Gamma }_{\boldsymbol{x}} \right)^{-1} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \otimes \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1}\), we obtain by the definition of \(\mathcal{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\boldsymbol{\pi} \boldsymbol{\pi}}(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)\) that \[\begin{equation*} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} \overset{as}{\sim} \mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{\pi},T^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \otimes \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1} \right)). \end{equation*}\] We do not consider the asymptotic distribution of \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\). However, we will later invoke the fact that \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u \overset{p}{\rightarrow} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\).

 

Extra: Some additional notes

The above result holds also without the assumption on stationary initial values provided the matrix \(T^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \otimes \boldsymbol{\Gamma }_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-1}\right)\) is replaced by the general formula of the Fisher information matrix \[\begin{equation*} \mathcal{\boldsymbol{I}}_{\boldsymbol{\pi} \boldsymbol{\pi}} (\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u^{-1} \otimes \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathsf{E}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t} \boldsymbol{x}_{t}^\prime\right). \end{equation*}\] The same point holds in the following where we consider restricted estimation and hypothesis testing (see below).

  • It is, however, useful to note that the accuracy of the asymptotic approximation in finite samples depends on the strength of the autocorrelation of the process.

  • When the stability condition of the VAR(\(p\)) is “nearly” invalid, the asymptotic approximation may be misleading in small (or even in fairly large) samples.

 

Restricted estimation of VAR(\(p\)). It is also important to note that parameter estimation can be carried out subject to linear constraints to parameters.

  • Details on this are available below as Extra material.

In summary, it can be concluded that estimation goes largely in the same (under the restrictions) as above and the resulting estimators have analogous (asymptotic) properties vs. the unrestricted case above. This is also important from the hypothesis testing perspective as introduced in the next Section.

 

Extra: Estimation subject to linear constraints

Consider the VAR(p) model (from the previous section), where the parameters \(\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{A}_{p}\) (and possibly \(\boldsymbol{\nu}\)) are subject to linear constraints. Such restrictions can be expressed conveniently so that the parameter \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) is assumed to satisfy \[\begin{equation} \boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{H} \boldsymbol{\delta} + \boldsymbol{a}, \end{equation}\] where \(\boldsymbol{H}\) is a known \(K(Kp+1)\times m\) matrix, \(\boldsymbol{a}\) a known \(K(Kp+1)\) vector and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) is an unknown \(m\times 1\) parameter vector. It is also assumed that \(\boldsymbol{H}\) is of full column rank, i.e., \(\mathsf{rank}(\boldsymbol{H})=m\).

It is quite common to express linear constraints by using the equation \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{R} \boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{b}, \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{R}\) \((q\times K(Kp+1)\times m)\) and \(\boldsymbol{b} \,\, (q\times 1)\) are known and \(\mathsf{rank}(\boldsymbol{R})=q\). Using linear algebra one can see that the two ways are equivalent, when \(q+m = K(Kp+1)\). The former is useful when we consider estimation (in this section) and the latter is useful when we consider a Wald test (the next section).

Most commonly we are interested in situations where some of the parameters are restricted to be zero. For example, if we choose \(\boldsymbol{H} = [\boldsymbol{I}_{K^{2}p}:\boldsymbol{0}]^\prime\) and \(\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{0}\), then the last \(K\) components of \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) are assume to be zero so that \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t-p}\) has no explanatory (or predictive) power for the last component \(y_{Kt}\) of \(\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\). In this case, \(\boldsymbol{R} =[\boldsymbol{0}:\boldsymbol{I}_{K}]\) and \(\boldsymbol{b} = \boldsymbol{0}\).

The log-likelihood function for a restricted model is obtained by replacing \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) by \(\boldsymbol{H} \boldsymbol{\delta} + \boldsymbol{a}\). Denote \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{z}_{t} = \boldsymbol{y}_{t} - \boldsymbol{X}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{a} \text{ and } \boldsymbol{W}_{t} = \boldsymbol{H}^\prime \boldsymbol{X}_{t}. \end{equation*}\] Then, the log-likelihood function \[\begin{equation*} l(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)=-\frac{T}{2}\log \left( \det (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u )\right) -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}-\boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\delta}\right)^\prime \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u \left(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}-\boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\delta} \right). \end{equation*}\] Differentiate this function with respect to the parameters. As in the case of the derivative, we get \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\delta} }l(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{W}_{t} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}-\boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\delta}\right). \end{equation*}\] Unlike in the case of \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\), the matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u\) cannot be moved to the front of the sum. For this reason the equation \(\partial l(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)\partial \boldsymbol{\delta} = \boldsymbol{0}\) cannot be solved without knowing \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), but instead the solution (usually) reduces to the form \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\delta} = \left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{W} _{t} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime\right)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{W}_{t} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u \boldsymbol{z}_{t}. \end{equation*}\] Using the notation \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\) we can repeat the derivations from the previous section and conclude that the first order condition for \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\), i.e. \(\partial l(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u )\partial \boldsymbol{\Phi} = \boldsymbol{0}\), leads to the equation \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u =\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left( \boldsymbol{z} _{t} -\boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\delta} \right)\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}-\boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime \boldsymbol{\delta}\right)^{\prime }. \end{equation*}\]

Given that the solution for \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) requires knowing \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) and the solution for \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) requires knowing \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\), numerical solution methods are required (except special cases). An illustrative method proceeds as follows. Replace the unknown \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) by its unconstrained estimate \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\) to yield \[\begin{equation*} \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(1)} = \left(\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{W}_{t} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u^{-1} \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime\right)^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^{-1} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u^{-1} \boldsymbol{z}_{t}. \end{equation*}\] This then yields a new estimate \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u^{(1)}\), which can again be used to replace \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\) in \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(1)}\). This results in a new estimate \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(2)}\) for \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\). This procedure can be continued until the estimates stop changing. This procedure is just an illustration. In practice, computer programs employ other algorithms. Nonetheless, the simple one step estimator \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{(1)}\) has good asymptotic properties, as it can be shown to be asymptotically equivalent to the true MLE (the reason being the orthogonality of \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\)).

Denote the ML estimators for \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) by \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\) and \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\) and derive their asymptotic distribution by relying on the general theory of ML estimation. Differentiate the log likelihood function with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) once more (see the derivation results in Appendix A) to obtain \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial \boldsymbol{\delta} \partial \boldsymbol{\delta}^\prime}l(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)=-\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{W}_{t}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime \end{equation*}\] so that the Fisher information matrix for \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) is \[\begin{equation*} \mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\delta\delta}}(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathsf{E} \left(\boldsymbol{W}_{t} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime\right), \end{equation*}\] which in the stationary case equals \(T \mathsf{E}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{t}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime\right)\). Similar calculations to those in the previous section show that \(\mathsf{E}\left(\partial^{2}l(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u)\partial \boldsymbol{\delta} \partial\sigma_{ij}\right)=0\) so that the parameters \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) are orthogonal. Hence, the asymptotic distribution of \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\) is \[\begin{equation*} \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}} \overset{as}{\sim} \mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{\delta},\mathcal{I}_{\boldsymbol{\delta\delta}}(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{-1}_u). \end{equation*}\] Here we can account for the fact that \(\boldsymbol{W}_{t} = \boldsymbol{H}^{\prime} \boldsymbol{X}_{t}\), where \(\boldsymbol{H}\) is known and fixed, but this does not result in any more illustrative formula.

On the part of the estimator \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\) for the constrained covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) it suffices to mention that it is consistent, which will be relied on later. Given what was noted above, we can write the estimator \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\) as \[\begin{equation*} \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}- \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^{\prime }\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{t}-\boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta} }\right)^\prime. \end{equation*}\]

 

7.2 Hypothesis testing

We derive the Wald test for the restriction \[\begin{equation*} H_{0}:\boldsymbol{R} \boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{b}, \end{equation*}\] which is assumed to hold in the following (when examining the null hypothesis). In addition, we assume the maximum likelihood estimators \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) and \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\) satisfy the asymptotic properties considered in unrestricted estimation.. Formally, the asymptotic normality of (assuming stationary initial values) is expressed as \[\begin{equation*} \sqrt{T}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} - \boldsymbol{\pi}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow } \mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \otimes \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{x}^{-1}). \end{equation*}\] As \(\boldsymbol{R} \boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{b}\), it follows from the linear transformation multivariate normal distribution (see Appendix B) that \[\begin{equation*} \sqrt{T}\left(\boldsymbol{R} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} - \boldsymbol{b}\right) = \boldsymbol{R} \sqrt{T}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} - \boldsymbol{\pi}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow} \boldsymbol{Z} \sim \mathsf{N}_{q}(\boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{R}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \otimes\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{x}^{-1}\right) \boldsymbol{R}^\prime), \end{equation*}\] and further that (see Appendix B) \[\begin{equation*} \sqrt{T}\left(\boldsymbol{R} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}} - \boldsymbol{b}\right)^\prime\left[\boldsymbol{R}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \otimes \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{x}^{-1}\right) \boldsymbol{R}^\prime\right]^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{R} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}- \boldsymbol{b}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow } \boldsymbol{Z}^\prime\left[\boldsymbol{R}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \otimes\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{x}^{-1}\right) \boldsymbol{R}^\prime\right]^{-1} \boldsymbol{Z} \sim \chi_{q}^{2} \end{equation*}\] where the last result follow from well known result on multivariate normal and the \(\chi^{2}\) distributions.

  • For clarity, here (and elsewhere if needed) the notation \(\mathsf{N}_{q}(\cdot,\cdot)\) signifies a multivariate normal distribution of dimension \(q\).

  • The mentioned result on multivariate normal and \(\chi^{2}\)-distribution states that if \(\boldsymbol{Z}\sim \mathsf{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\), then \(\left(\boldsymbol{Z}- \boldsymbol{\mu}\right)^\prime \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)\sim\chi_{k}^{2}\) (assuming \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is positive definite).

The square form on the left hand side measures the realism of the restriction, but contains the unknown matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \otimes \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{x}^{-1}\), which has to be replaced by a consistent estimator in a test.

  • As \(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{x} = \mathsf{E}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t} \boldsymbol{x}_{t}^\prime)\), where \(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=[1 \quad \boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}^\prime]^\prime=[1 \,\, \boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}^\prime\ \cdots \boldsymbol{y}_{t-p}^\prime ]^\prime\), and given the general consistency results for the first and second order moments of a stationary process together with the consistency of \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\), allows us to conclude the result \[\begin{equation*} \left[\boldsymbol{R} \left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u \otimes \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{x}^{-1}\right) \boldsymbol{R}^\prime\right]^{-1}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\left[\boldsymbol{R}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u \otimes \boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{x}^{-1}\right) \boldsymbol{R}^\prime\right]^{-1}, \end{equation*}\] where \[\begin{equation*} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{x}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{x}_{t} \boldsymbol{x}_{t}^\prime. \end{equation*}\]

In conclusion, we then obtain the Wald test statistic (see Appendix B) \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{W}=T\left(\boldsymbol{R}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}-\boldsymbol{b}\right)^\prime\left[\boldsymbol{R}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u \otimes \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{x}^{-1}\right) \boldsymbol{R}^\prime\right]^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{R}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}-\boldsymbol{b}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\chi_{q}^{2} \end{equation*}\] Large values of the test statistic are critical about the null hypothesis (\(H_{0}:\boldsymbol{R} \boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{b}\)). In practice, we apply the test by computing the approximate p-value \[\begin{equation*} p=\Pr\left\{\mathsf{W}\geq \mathsf{W}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\}\approx\Pr \left\{\chi_{q}^{2}\geq\mathsf{W}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\}, \end{equation*}\] where \(\chi_{q}^{2}\) indicates a \(\chi_{q}^{2}\)-distributed random variable and \(\mathsf{W}(\boldsymbol{y})\) is the value of the test statistic based on the observed time series data to be analyzed.

 

Likelihood ratio (LR) test. Instead of the Wald test, one can apply the likelihood ratio (LR) statistic. The general formula is \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{LR}=2\left[l(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u)-l(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u)\right], \end{equation*}\] where \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\) and \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u\) are the constrained maximum likelihood estimators for \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_u\) (see the previous section). Using the formulae of the log-likelihood functions one can conclude by direct calculation that \[\begin{equation*} l(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u) = -\frac{T}{2}\log\det(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u)-\frac{TK}{2} \quad \mathrm{and} \quad l(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u) = -\frac{T}{2}\log\det(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u)-\frac{TK}{2} \end{equation*}\] Hence, we get the LR test statistic \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{LR} = 2\left[\log\det(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u)-\log\det (\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_u)\right]\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\chi_{q}^{2} \end{equation*}\] where the asymptotic distribution can be concluded by the arguments as in the case of the Wald test. Large values of the test statistic are critical to the null hypothesis and approximate p-values are obtained as in the Wald test.

 

Extra: Properties of the constrained ML estimator

The above test statistics can be generalized to cover the parameter \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) of the restricted VAR(\(p\)) model (see the restriction of interest). The constrained ML estimator was seen to obey the asymptotic normal distribution as \[\begin{equation*} \sqrt{T}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}} - \boldsymbol{\delta}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow } \mathsf{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathsf{E}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{t}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime\right)\right)^{-1}\right) \end{equation*}\] In order to apply the Wald test we need a valid empirical counterpart for the matrix \(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathsf{E}\left(\boldsymbol{W}_{t} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_u \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime\right)\). One obvious alternative is \(T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{W}_{t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}_u \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^{\prime}\), which allows one to derive the Wald test as above in the case of unconstrained ML estimates. The square roots of the diagonal elements of \(\sum_{t=1}^{T} \boldsymbol{W}_{t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}_u \boldsymbol{W}_{t}^\prime\) yield approximates for standard errors for the components of \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\). Let \(s.e.(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{i})\) denote the standard error of the \(i\):th component of \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\). Then \[\begin{equation*} \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{i}/s.e.(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{i})\overset{as}{\sim} \mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i},1) \end{equation*}\] so that the approximate \(95\%\) confidence interval for \(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}\) is \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{i}\pm 1.96s.e.(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{i})\).

In the same way, one can form confidence intervals for the elements of the unconstrained parameter \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\).
