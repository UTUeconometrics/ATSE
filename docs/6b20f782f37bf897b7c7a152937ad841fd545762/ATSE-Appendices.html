Appendix A: Matrix calculus

Matrix Norm

For a matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}=[a_{ij}]\) of dimension \(n\times m\) the Euclidean norm is \[\begin{equation*} \left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{ij}^{2}} \end{equation*}\] The same norm can be expressed by applying the standard Euclidean norm to the \(nm\times 1\) vector whose elements constitute the elements of \(\boldsymbol{A}\). Define the vectorization operator \(\mathsf{Vec}(\cdot)\) as follows. Partition \(\boldsymbol{A}=[\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\ \cdots \ \boldsymbol{a}_{m}]\) by columns so that \(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\) \((n\times 1)\) is the \(i\):th column of \(\boldsymbol{A}\) and define the vector (the vectorized \(\boldsymbol{A}\)) as \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{Vec}(\boldsymbol{A})=\left[ \begin{array}{c} \boldsymbol{a}_{1} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{a}_{m} \end{array} \right] \ \ \ (nm\times 1) \end{equation*}\] Then it is clear that \(\left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert\) is the norm of \(\mathsf{Vec}(\boldsymbol{A})\), that is, \[\begin{equation*} \left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert =\sqrt{\mathsf{vec}(\boldsymbol{A})^{\prime}\mathsf{vec}(\boldsymbol{A})} \end{equation*}\]

The latter definition and known properties of a vector norm enable us to see that the matrix norm has the same properties as the vector norm

  • \(\left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert \geq 0\) and \(\left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert = 0\) if and only if \(\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{0}\)

  • \(\left\Vert c\boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert =\left\vert c\right\vert \left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert\) for all \(c\in\mathbb{R}\)

  • \(\left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{B}\right\Vert \leq \left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert+\left\Vert \boldsymbol{B}\right\Vert\) for all \(m\times n\) matrices \(\boldsymbol{B}\) (triangular inequality)

  • \(\left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\right\Vert \leq \left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert\left\Vert \boldsymbol{B}\right\Vert\) (submultiplicativity)

A variety of other matrix norms are applied that satisfy the above four conditions (sometimes only the first three ones), but these norms are not needed in this course.

Sometimes it is useful to express the Euclidean norm by applying the trace of a square matrix, i.e., the sum of diagonal elements. If \(\boldsymbol{B}=[b_{ij}]\) is \(n\times n\) matrix, then its trace \(\mathsf{tr}(\boldsymbol{B})\) is defined by \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{tr}(\boldsymbol{B})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{ii} \end{equation*}\] It is easy to see from the definition that \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{tr}(\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{C})=\mathsf{tr}(\boldsymbol{B})+\mathsf{tr}(\boldsymbol{C})\text{ and }\mathsf{tr}(\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{D})=\mathsf{tr}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{B}) \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{C}\) is \(n\times n\) matrix, while in the latter condition \(\boldsymbol{B}\) can be \(n\times m\) and \(\boldsymbol{D}\) can be \(m\times n\).

The norm of the matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}\) (\(n\times m\)) can be written \[\begin{equation*} \left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert =\sqrt{\mathsf{tr}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{A})} \end{equation*}\] Appendix Matrix Sequences and Series shows how the matrix norm can be used to analyze the convergence of matrix sequences and series.

Matrix Eigenvalues and Jordan Decomposition

The eigenvalues \(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda _{n}\) of a square matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}\) \((n\times n)\) satisfy (are solutions to) the determinant equation \[\begin{equation*} \det (\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda \boldsymbol{I}_{n})=0 \end{equation*}\] The corresponding eigenvectors \(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\) (\(\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\neq \boldsymbol{0}\)) are obtained from the equations \[\begin{equation*} (\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{I}_{n})\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}\Longleftrightarrow \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=\lambda _{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}, \quad i=1,...,n \end{equation*}\]

If \(\boldsymbol{A}\) \((n\times n)\) and \(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda _{n}\) are as above, there exists a non-singular matrix \(\boldsymbol{P}\) \((n\times n)\) such that \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda} \boldsymbol{P}^{-1} \end{equation*}\] Here \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) \((n\times n)\) is the block diagonal matrix \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Lambda} =\left[ \begin{array}{cccc} \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n_{1}} & \boldsymbol{0} & \cdots & \boldsymbol{0} \\ \boldsymbol{0} & \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n_{2}} & \cdots & \boldsymbol{0} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{0} & \boldsymbol{0} & \cdots & \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n_{k}} \end{array} \right] \equiv \mathsf{diag}[\boldsymbol{\Lambda} _{n_{1}}\ \cdots \ \boldsymbol{\Lambda} _{n_{k}}] \end{equation*}\] where \(n_{1}+\cdots +n_{k}=n\) and \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Lambda} _{n_{i}}=\left[ \begin{array}{cccccc} \lambda_{i} & 1 & 0 & \cdots & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_{i} & 1 & & & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & & \vdots \\ \vdots & & & \ddots & & 0 \\ \vdots & & & 0 & \lambda_{i} & 1 \\ 0 & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & 0 & \lambda_{i} \end{array} \right] \end{equation*}\] The cells on the main diagonal of \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{n_{i}}\) contain the eigenvalue \(\lambda_{i}\), the cells of the diagonal right above the main diagonal contain \(1\) and all other cells contain \(0\) (the same eigenvalue may appear in several blocks of \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\)). This representation of matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is called the or the . If the eigenvalues of \(\boldsymbol{A}\) are distinct, the matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) is diagonal and its diagonal elements are the eigenvalues of \(\boldsymbol{A}\), while the columns of \(\boldsymbol{P}\) (that are linearly independent) are the eigenvectors of \(\boldsymbol{A}\).

By computing we can show the following useful result (where \(\binom{k}{j}=0\), if \(j>k\)) \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{\Lambda} _{n_{i}}^{k}=\left[ \begin{array}{cccccc} \lambda _{i}^{k} & \binom{k}{1}\lambda _{i}^{k-1} & \cdots & \binom{k}{j} \lambda _{i}^{k-j} & \cdots & \binom{k}{n_{i}-1}\lambda _{i}^{k-n_{i}+1} \\ 0 & \ddots & \ddots & & \ddots & \\ \vdots & 0 & \ddots & \ddots & & \ddots \\ \vdots & & 0 & \ddots & \ddots & \\ \vdots & & & 0 & \ddots & \ddots \\ 0 & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & 0 & \ddots \end{array} \right] , \end{equation*}\] where each diagonal carries the same element (and all elements below the main diagonal are zeros).

Matrix Sequences and Series

The convergence of a matrix sequence \(\boldsymbol{A}_{N}=[a_{ij,N}]\), \(N=1,2,...\) to the matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}=[a_{ij}]\) (of the same dimension) may be defined to require that \(a_{ij,N}\rightarrow a_{ij}\) for all \(i,j\), as \(N\rightarrow \infty\). This is denoted by \(\lim_{N\rightarrow \infty }\boldsymbol{A}_{N}=\boldsymbol{A}\) or \(\boldsymbol{A}_{N}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{A}\) (as \(N\rightarrow \infty\)).

The convergence of a matrix series \(\boldsymbol{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}\) is defined as the limit of the sequence of partial sums \(\boldsymbol{S}_{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}\), that is, there exists a matrix \(\boldsymbol{S}\) such that \(\boldsymbol{S}_{N}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{S}\), which implies that \(\boldsymbol{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty }\boldsymbol{A}_{i}\).

If \(\boldsymbol{A}=[a_{ij}]\) is \(n\times m\) matrix, then the definition of the matrix norm implies \(\max \left\{\left\vert a_{ij}\right\vert \right\} \leq \left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert \leq \sqrt{nm}\max \left\{ \left\vert a_{ij}\right\vert \right\}\), where maximization is over \(i=1,...,n\) and \(j=1,...,m\). Using this together with the definition of convergence of real sequences we observe that for the matrix sequence \(\boldsymbol{A}_{N}\) it holds that \(\boldsymbol{A}_{N}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{A}\) if and only if \(\left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}_{N}-\boldsymbol{A}\right\Vert \rightarrow 0\). An analogous alternative definition can also be presented for matrix series. Given the above notes and well known results on the convergence of real valued series it is clear that a sufficient condition for a matrix series to converge is that the real valued series \(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty }\left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}_{i}\right\Vert\) converges (absolute convergence).

Let \(\boldsymbol{A}\) be an \(n\times n\) square matrix and \(\lambda _{1},...,\lambda _{n}\) its eigenvalues. Assume \(\rho \equiv \max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\vert \lambda_{i}\right\vert <1\). By applying the Jordan decomposition, we obtain \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda} \boldsymbol{P}^{-1})^{N}=\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda} ^{N}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1} \end{equation*}\] Because \(N^{k}\rho^{N}\rightarrow 0\), as \(N\rightarrow\infty\), for all \(k\geq 0\), we obtain using results from Section A.2 that \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{N}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{0}\) and hence, given the above equation, we have \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}^{N}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{0}\text{,\ as }N\rightarrow \infty \end{equation*}\] Choose \(0<\overline{\lambda }<1\) such that \(\rho <\overline{\lambda }<1\) (we can always find such \(\overline{\lambda }\)). Then, \(N^{k}\rho^{N}=N^{k}\left(\rho /\overline{\lambda }\right)^{N}\overline{\lambda }^{N}\), where \(N^{k}\left( \rho /\overline{\lambda }\right)^{N}\rightarrow 0\). Hence \(N^{k}\rho^{N}<\overline{\lambda}^{N}\) for all sufficiently large \(N\). It follows that the convergence \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{N}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{0}\) is geometric, that is, there exists a constant \(C< \infty\) and a constant \(r<1\) such that for the matrix elements \([\boldsymbol{\Lambda} ^{N}]_{ij}\) it holds that \([\boldsymbol{\Lambda} ^{N}]_{ij}\leq Cr^{N}\). The same applies to the elements of the matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}^{N}=\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda} ^{N}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\). By the submultiplicativity of a matrix it also follows that \[\begin{equation*} \left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}^{N}\right\Vert =\left\Vert \boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda} ^{N}\boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\right\Vert \leq\left\Vert \boldsymbol{P}\right\Vert \left\Vert \boldsymbol{P}^{-1}\right\Vert \left\Vert \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{N}\right\Vert \end{equation*}\] so that \(\left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}^{N}\right\Vert \rightarrow 0\) geometrically (If the eigenvalues of \(\boldsymbol{A}\) are distinct, $ $ is diagonal with diagonal elements among the eigenvalues of \(\boldsymbol{A}\) that are smaller than one in absolute value. Hence the argument behind the result becomes simpler). Altogether we can conclude that \(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}\) converges (\(\boldsymbol{A}^{0}=\boldsymbol{I}_{n}\)) and it is easily seen that \[\begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}=\left(\boldsymbol{I}_{n}-\boldsymbol{A}\right)^{-1} \end{equation*}\]

Polynomials, Polynomial Matrices and Power Series

Consider the \(m\):th degree (or order) polynomial function \[\begin{equation*} p(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}z^{k} \end{equation*}\] where \(a_{k}\) and \(z\) are real or complex numbers and \(a_{m}\neq 0\). When \(z\in \mathbb{C}\), the fundamental theorem of algebra implies that we can write \[\begin{equation*} p(z)=a_{m}\left( z-\zeta _{1}\right) \cdots \left( z-\zeta _{m}\right) , \end{equation*}\] where \(\zeta _{1},...,\zeta _{m}\) are the roots of \(p(z)\) such that \(p(\zeta_{k})=0\). The case \(\zeta _{k}=\zeta _{l}\) (\(k\neq l\)) is possible and when the coefficients \(a_{k}\) are real (\(a_{k}\in \mathbb{R}\) for all \(k\)) possible complex roots appear as conjugate pairs so that if \(\zeta_{k}=x_{k}+iy_{k}\) (\(i^{2}=-1\)) then for some \(l\neq k\), \(\zeta_{l}=x_{l}-iy_{l}\equiv \overline{\zeta}_{l}\).

A is a matrix whose elements are polynomials \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{P}(z)=[p_{ij}(z)],\ \ p_{ij}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m_{ij}}a_{ij,k}z^{k} \end{equation*}\] Setting \(m=\max \left\{ m_{ij}\right\}\) we can write \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{P}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}z^{k}, \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{A}_{k}=[a_{ij,k}]\) and \(a_{ij,k}=0\), when \(k>m_{ij}\). Given this presentation it is customary to talk about matrix polynomials, that is, polynomials whose coefficients are matrices.

Calculations such as \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)+\boldsymbol{B}(z)\), \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)\boldsymbol{B}(z)\) and the determinant $((z)) $ are defined as in the case of usual matrices. For a square matrix polynomial \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)\) \((n\times n)\) the inverse matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)^{-1}\) \((n\times n)\) satisfies \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)\boldsymbol{A}(z)^{-1}=\boldsymbol{I}_{n}\). As in the case of matrix whose elements are real numbers, we can compute the inverse matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)^{-1}\) by the formula \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}(z)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det (\boldsymbol{A}(z))}\boldsymbol{A}(z)^{\ast }\ \ (\det (\boldsymbol{A})\neq 0) \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)^{\ast }=[a_{ij}^{\ast}(z)]\) is the adjunct matrix of \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)\) such that \(a_{ij}^{\ast }(z)=(-1)^{i+j}\times \det (\boldsymbol{M}_{ij})\), where \(\boldsymbol{M}_{ij}\) is the \((n-1)\times (n-1)\) matrix that results from deleting row \(i\) and column \(j\) from \(\boldsymbol{A}\) (the same definition applies to the usual matrix). Given that a determinant can be formed by summing products of elements of the matrix, both \(\det(\boldsymbol{A}(z))\) and \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)^{\ast }\) are polynomials and the degree of \(\det (\boldsymbol{A}(z))\) is at most \(mn\). Hence, the elements of the inverse matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}(z)^{-1}\) are rational functions of the form \(\sum_{j=0}^{q}a_{j}z^{j}/\sum_{j=0}^{r}b_{j}z^{j}\ (q,r<\infty )\).

Return to the usual polynomial \(p(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}z^{k}\). It is easy to see that we can write \[ p(z)=p(1)+(1-z)q(z) \tag{1}\] where \[\begin{equation*} q(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}b_{k}z^{k},\ \ b_{k}=-\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}a_{j}, \ k=1,...,m-1 \end{equation*}\] Another similar representation is \[ p(z)=p(1)z+(1-z)r(z) \tag{2}\] where \[\begin{equation*} r(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}c_{k}z^{k},\ c_{0}=a_{0},\ c_{k}=-\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}a_{j},\ k=1,...,m-1 \end{equation*}\] These representations can be generalized to the case of power series \[\begin{equation*} p(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty }a_{k}z^{k}\ \ \ (\left\vert z\right\vert \leq 1) \end{equation*}\] If we assume \[\begin{equation*} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty }k\left\vert a_{k}\right\vert <\infty \end{equation*}\] it can be easily proved that equations Equation 1 and Equation 2 hold when \(\left\vert z\right\vert \leq 1\) and \(m\) is replaced by \(\infty\). Furthermore, it holds that \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\vert b_{k}\right\vert < \infty\) and \(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\vert c_{k}\right\vert <\infty\).

The above stated results generalize to matrix polynomials. Let \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{P}(z)=[p_{ij}(z)]=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}z^{k}\ \ \ (\left\vert z\right\vert \leq 1) \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{A}_{k}=[a_{ij,k}]\) satisfies the condition \[\begin{equation*} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty }k\left\Vert \boldsymbol{A}_{k}\right\Vert <\infty \end{equation*}\] By applying the above derivations to the polynomials \(p_{ij}(z)\) we can show the following generalization of Equation 1 \[\begin{equation}\label{eq:PzP1} \boldsymbol{P}(z)=\boldsymbol{P}(1)z+(1-z)\boldsymbol{Q}(z) \end{equation}\] where \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{Q}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty }\boldsymbol{B}_{k}z^{k},\ \boldsymbol{B}_{k}=-\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty }\boldsymbol{A}_{j} \text{ and }\sum_{k=1}^{\infty }\left\Vert \boldsymbol{B}_{k}\right\Vert <\infty \end{equation*}\] Similarly we can generalize Equation 2 to \[\begin{equation} \boldsymbol{P}(z)=\boldsymbol{P}(1)z+(1-z)\boldsymbol{R}(z) \end{equation}\] where \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{R}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty }\boldsymbol{C}_{k}z^{k},\ \boldsymbol{C}_{0}=\boldsymbol{A}_{0},\ \boldsymbol{C}_{k}=-\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty }\boldsymbol{A}_{j},\ k\geq 1\ \text{and }\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\Vert \boldsymbol{C}_{k}\right\Vert <\infty \end{equation*}\] If \(\boldsymbol{P}(z)\) is degree \(m\) matrix polynomial (i.e., \(\boldsymbol{A}_{k}=\boldsymbol{0}\) for all \(k>m\)), then \(\boldsymbol{Q}(z)\) and \(\boldsymbol{R}(z)\) are degree \(m-1\) matrix polynomials.

Partitioned Matrices and the Kronecker Product

A partitioned matrix is a matrix whose elements are matrices \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}=[\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}]=\left[ \begin{array}{ccc} \boldsymbol{A}_{11} & \cdots & \boldsymbol{A}_{1m} \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{A}_{n1} & \cdots & \boldsymbol{A}_{nm} \end{array} \right] \end{equation*}\] If \(\boldsymbol{A}=[\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}]\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}=[\boldsymbol{B}_{ij}]\), then (assuming that the dimensions agree with the definitions of the calculations) \[\begin{equation*} \boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{B}=[\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}+\boldsymbol{B}_{ij}]\text{ and }\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}=\left[\sum_{k}\boldsymbol{A}_{ik}\boldsymbol{B}_{kj}\right] \end{equation*}\] These results follow from standard matrix addition and multiplication operations. In addition, when \(\det (\boldsymbol{A})\neq 0\) and \(\det (\boldsymbol{A}_{ii})\neq 0\ (i=1,2)\) we have \[\begin{equation*} \left[ \begin{array}{cc} \boldsymbol{A}_{11} & \boldsymbol{A}_{12} \\ \boldsymbol{A}_{21} & \boldsymbol{A}_{22} \end{array}% \right] ^{-1}=\left[ \begin{array}{cc} \boldsymbol{A}_{1\cdot 2}^{-1} & -\boldsymbol{A}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{12}\boldsymbol{A}_{2\cdot 1}^{-1} \\ -\boldsymbol{A}_{22}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{21}\boldsymbol{A}_{1\cdot 2}^{-1} & \boldsymbol{A}_{2\cdot 1}^{-1} \end{array} \right] , \end{equation*}\] where \(\boldsymbol{A}_{i\cdot j}=\boldsymbol{A}_{ii}-\boldsymbol{A}_{ij}\boldsymbol{A}_{jj}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{ji}\). It also holds that (a) \(\det (\boldsymbol{A})=\det (\boldsymbol{A}_{11})\det (\boldsymbol{A}_{22})\), when \(\boldsymbol{A}_{12}=\boldsymbol{0}\) or \(\boldsymbol{A}_{21}=\boldsymbol{0}\), (b) \(\det (\boldsymbol{A})=\det (\boldsymbol{A}_{11})\det (\boldsymbol{A}_{2\cdot 1})\), when \(\det (\boldsymbol{A}_{11})\neq 0\), and (c) \(\det (\boldsymbol{A})=\det (\boldsymbol{A}_{22})\det (\boldsymbol{A}_{1\cdot 2})\), when \(\det (\boldsymbol{A}_{22})\neq 0\).

Properties of Conditional Expectation

Consider the prediction problem where we seek to predict the value of a random (scalar) variable \(Y\) given the value \(\boldsymbol{x}\) of a random vector \(\boldsymbol{X}\). Probability theory tells us that the optimal in the sense of mean square error prediction is obtained by the conditional expectation (conditional mean) of \(Y\) given \(\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}\), denoted \(\mathsf{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})\). That is, for any function \(g(\boldsymbol{X})\) it holds that \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{E}\left[\left(Y-\mathsf{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{2}\right]\leq \mathsf{E}\left[\left(Y-g(\boldsymbol{X})\right)^{2}\right] \end{equation*}\] When \(\boldsymbol{X}\) and \(Y\) are jointly continuous, the conditional mean is \[\begin{equation*} \mathsf{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{-\infty }^{\infty}yf_{Y|\boldsymbol{X}}(y;\boldsymbol{x})dy=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}y\frac{f_{Y,\boldsymbol{X}}(y,\boldsymbol{x})}{f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})}dy, \end{equation*}\] where \(f_{Y,\boldsymbol{X}}(y,\boldsymbol{x})\) is the joint density of \((Y,\boldsymbol{X})\), \(f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{-\infty }^{\infty}f_{Y,\boldsymbol{X}}(y,\boldsymbol{x})dy\) is the marginal density of \(\boldsymbol{X}\), and \(f_{Y|\boldsymbol{X}}(y;\boldsymbol{x})=f_{Y,\boldsymbol{X}}(y,\boldsymbol{x})/f_{\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})\) is the density of \(Y\) conditional on \(\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}\). These definition as well as the ones that follow hold also when \(\boldsymbol{Y}\) is a random vector.

The conditional mean has alternative interpretations. As such, \(\mathsf{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})\) returns the mean of a conditional distribution (\(f_{Y|\boldsymbol{X}}(y|\boldsymbol{x})\)), a certain non-random number. On the other hand, \(\mathsf{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})=m(\boldsymbol{x})\) is a non-random function that returns the conditional mean of \(Y\) for any given value \(\boldsymbol{x}\) of \(\boldsymbol{X}\). The conditional mean can also be regarded as a random variable when the value of the conditioning variable \(\boldsymbol{X}\) is not thought as fixed. In this case, the conditional mean can be seen as a transformation \(m(\boldsymbol{X})\) of the random variable \(\boldsymbol{X}\), where the transformation \(m(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathsf{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})\). We may write \(\mathsf{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{X})\) to indicate that the conditional mean is regarded as a random variable.

In advanced probability theory conditional expectation is defined directly as a random variable and the conditioning random vector variable \(\boldsymbol{X}\) can be of infinite dimension. This type of situation comes across when we seek to predict a random process assuming that the past values (prior to the date of forecast) of the process \(\{y_{t},\ t=0,\pm 1,...\}\) are available. For us it suffices to know that the following basic properties of the conditional mean hold also in such a general situation.

  • CE1 \(\mathsf{E}(aY_{1}+bY_{2}|\boldsymbol{X})=a\mathsf{E}(Y_{1}|\boldsymbol{X})+b\mathsf{E}(Y_{2}|\boldsymbol{X})\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are nonrandom constants (linearity)

  • CE2 \(\mathsf{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{X})=\mathsf{E}(Y)\), when \(Y\) and \(\boldsymbol{X}\) are independent

  • CE3 $(Y)=$, (law of iterated expectation)

  • CE4 \(\mathsf{E}(g(\boldsymbol{X})Y|\boldsymbol{X})=g(\boldsymbol{X})\mathsf{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{X})\) for any function \(g\) (such that \(g(\boldsymbol{X})Y\) is a well defined random variable with finite expectation) \end{itemize}

Differentiation Rules

When \(f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) is differentiable, we denote \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}f(\boldsymbol{x})=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\ \cdots \ \frac{\partial }{\partial x_{n}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\right] ^{\prime }\ \ (n\times 1) \end{equation*}\] and \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{x}^{\prime }}f(\boldsymbol{x})=\left[ \frac{\partial }{\partial x_{1}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\ \cdots \ \frac{\partial }{\partial x_{n}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\right] ^{\prime }\ \ (1\times n) \end{equation*}\] When \(\boldsymbol{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{m}\) is differentiable, we denote \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left[ \begin{array}{ccc} \frac{\partial }{\partial x_{1}}f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}) & \cdots & \frac{\partial }{\partial x_{1}}f_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}) \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial }{\partial x_{n}}f_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}) & \cdots & \frac{\partial }{\partial x_{n}}f_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}) \end{array} \right] \end{equation*}\]

It is easy to check that \(\partial \boldsymbol{a}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{x}/\partial \boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{a}\ (n\times 1)\) and \(\partial \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}/\partial \boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime }\), when \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is \(m\times n\). Furthermore, when \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is \(n\times n\) we have \[\begin{align*} \frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x} &= (\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime })\boldsymbol{x} \\ &= 2\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x},\text{\ if }\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime } \end{align*}\] When \(\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{B}^{\prime}\) is \(m\times m\) we obtain using the above rules that \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{x}}\left( \boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right) ^{\prime }\boldsymbol{B}\left( \boldsymbol{c}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right)=-2\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{c}+2\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\ \ \ \text{(}\boldsymbol{c}\ m\times 1\text{\ and }\boldsymbol{A} m\times n\text{)} \end{equation*}\]

Suppose now that \(\boldsymbol{A}\) (\(n\times n\)) is nonsymmetric and unrestricted (that is, the value \(a_{ij}\) is unrelated to the value \(a_{kl}\), when either \(i\neq k\) or \(j\neq l\)). Then \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\equiv \left[ \frac{\partial }{\partial a_{ij}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right] =[x_{i}x_{j}]=\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime }\ \ \text{(}\boldsymbol{x}\ n\times 1\text{\ and }\boldsymbol{A}\ n\times n\text{)} \end{equation*}\] and \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{A}}\log\det(\boldsymbol{A})=\left( \boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right)^{\prime}=\left( \boldsymbol{A}^{\prime }\right)^{-1} \end{equation*}\] The latter result uses the fact that \(\det (\boldsymbol{A})=\sum_{k}a_{ik}a_{ik}^{\ast }\), where \(a_{ik}^{\ast }\), the element of the adjoint matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}^{\ast}\) of \(\boldsymbol{A}\) (see Appendix Polynomials, Polynomial Matrices and Power Series, does not depend on \(a_{ij}\). By the chain rule we get \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial }{\partial a_{ij}}\log \det (\boldsymbol{A})=\frac{1}{\det (\boldsymbol{A})}\frac{\partial \det (\boldsymbol{A})}{\partial a_{ij}}=\frac{1}{\det (\boldsymbol{A})}a_{ij}^{\ast}=[\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}]_{ji}=\left[ \left( \boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right) ^{\prime }\right]_{ij} \end{equation*}\] where the third equation uses the formula of the inverse matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}=\left(\det (\boldsymbol{A})\right) ^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{\ast}\) and the definition of the adjoint matrix (see Appendix Polynomials, Polynomial Matrices and Power Series).

The last two results above do not hold when $ $is symmetric. If \(\boldsymbol{A}(n\times n)\) is symmetric \(\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\prime}\), then \(a_{ij}=a_{ji}\) and we obtain \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial a_{ij}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=\left\{ \begin{array}{c} x_{i}^{2}\text{, when }i=j \\ 2x_{i}x_{j}\text{, when }i\neq j \end{array} \right. \end{equation*}\] and \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial a_{ij}}\log(\det(\boldsymbol{A}))=\left\{ \begin{array}{c} \left[ \boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right] _{ii}\text{, when }i=j \\ 2\left[ \boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right] _{ji}\text{, when }i\neq j \end{array} \right. \end{equation*}\] Using matrix notation these formulae can be expressed as \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}=2\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime }-\mathsf{diag}\left[\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime }\right] \end{equation*}\] and \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{A}}\log (\det (\boldsymbol{A}))=2\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}-\mathsf{diag}\left[\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\right] \end{equation*}\] where $$ indicates a diagonal matrix whose diagonal elements are those of \(\boldsymbol{A}\) (i.e., \(\mathsf{diag}\left[ \boldsymbol{A}\right]=[a_{ij}I(i=j)]_{ij}\), where \(I(i=j)=1\), if \(i=j\) and \(I(i=j)=0\), \(i\neq j\)).

Apply the above results on the log likelihood function (of Section (Linear constraints) \[\begin{equation*} l(\boldsymbol{\pi} ,\boldsymbol{\Sigma} )=\frac{T}{2}\log \left( \det (\boldsymbol{\Phi} )\right) -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{u} _{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi} )^{\prime }\boldsymbol{\Phi} \boldsymbol{u} _{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi} ) \end{equation*}\] We get \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{\Phi} }\boldsymbol{u} _{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi} )^{\prime }\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{u}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi})=2\boldsymbol{u} _{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi} )\boldsymbol{u}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi})^{\prime}-\mathsf{diag}\left[\boldsymbol{u} _{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi} )\boldsymbol{u} _{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi} )^{\prime }\right] \end{equation*}\] and (using the notation \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\)) \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{\Phi} }\log \left( \det (\boldsymbol{\Phi} )\right) =2\boldsymbol{\Phi} ^{-1}-\mathsf{diag}\left[ \boldsymbol{\Phi} ^{-1}\right] =2\boldsymbol{\Sigma} -\mathsf{diag}\left[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \right] \end{equation*}\] Now, the first order condition with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\) of the likelihood function is seen to be \[\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial }{\partial \boldsymbol{\Phi} }l(\boldsymbol{\pi} ,\boldsymbol{\Sigma} ) = \frac{T}{2}\left\{ Appendix B: Asymptotic results

We briefly review selected results on stochastic convergence and convergence in distribution (the underlying concepts of convergence are assumed known). The first results concerns the behavior of stochastic convergence when a continuous transformation is applied. Here and later a constant means a nonrandom finite quantity.

Theorem B.1. If the sequence of random variables \(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{X}_{2},...\ (k\times 1)\) converges in stochastics to a constant vector \(\boldsymbol{c}\), i.e., \(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{c}\), and if \(\boldsymbol{g}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{l}\) is continuous at \(\boldsymbol{c}\), then \(\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\overset{p}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{c})\).

This result holds also for matrices. The matrix case can be handled by using vectorization (see Appendix Matrix Norm). The same applies to the following variants of the continuous mapping theorem.

Theorem B.2. If the sequence of random variables \(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{X}_{2},...\ (k\times 1)\) converges in distribution to a random vector \(\boldsymbol{Z}\), i.e., \(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{Z}\), and if \(\boldsymbol{g}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{l}\) is continuous, then \(\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{Z})\).

Here the continuity condition can be weakened. It suffices that \(\boldsymbol{g}\) is continuous within the set \(C_{g}\) such that \(\Pr \left\{ \boldsymbol{Z}\in C_{g}\right\} =1\). Given that convergence in distribution to a constant implies stochastic convergence, Theorem B.2 implies Theorem B.1 when \(\boldsymbol{Z}=\boldsymbol{c}\)

  • Convergence to distribution follows always from stochastic convergence but the reverse is true only when there is a constant limit for the convergence to distribution.

The following theorem contains the above two theorems as special cases.

Theorem B.3. Assume the sequences \(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{X}_{2},...\ (k\times 1)\) and \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1},\boldsymbol{Y}_{2},...(l\times 1)\) are such that \(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{Z}\) and \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{c}\), where \(\boldsymbol{c}\) is a constant vector. Then, \((\boldsymbol{X}_{n},\boldsymbol{Y}_{n})\overset{d}{\rightarrow }(\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{c})\).

The above theorems imply the following results.

The first result holds also for vectors.

Corollary B.2. Let \(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{X}_{2},...\ \)and \(\boldsymbol{Z} (k\times 1)\) be as in Theorem B.3 and let \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1},\boldsymbol{Y}_{2},...\ (l\times 1)\) be a sequence of \(l\times k\) matrices such that \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{A}\), where \(\boldsymbol{A} (l\times k)\) is a constant matrix. Then

  1. : \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}\)

If \(k=l\), it also holds that

  1. : \(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}\)
  2. : \(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{Z}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{Z}\), when \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is nonsingular (i.e., invertible).

These results follow from the continuity of the functions \((\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x})\longmapsto \boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\), \((\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{x})\longmapsto \boldsymbol{x}^{\prime }\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\) and \(\boldsymbol{A}\longmapsto \boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\) (\(\boldsymbol{A}\) nonsingular) and Theorems B.2 and B.3. When \(\boldsymbol{Z}\) is a constant, we can replace convergence in distribution by (the stronger condition of) stochastic convergence. The continuity of an inverse matrix can be justified as follows. The determinant function \(\boldsymbol{A}\longmapsto \det (\boldsymbol{A})\) is continuous, because the determinant can be formed by summing products of matrix elements. As the elements of \(\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\) can be expressed in terms of the ratios of \(\boldsymbol{A}\) and the determinants of its submatrices (see Appendices A.4 and A.7), the function \(\boldsymbol{A}\longmapsto \boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\) is a composition function of continuous functions and hence continuous.

Corollary 1 (iii) assumes implicitly that \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\neq \boldsymbol{0}\) for all \(n\) (similarly Corollary 1 (iii) assumes implicitly that \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\) is nonsingular for all \(n\)). Although the assumption \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\overset{p}{\rightarrow }\boldsymbol{c}\neq \boldsymbol{0}\) implies that \(\Pr \left\{ \boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\neq \boldsymbol{0}\right\}\) tends to \(0\) (as \(n\rightarrow \infty\)), the condition \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\neq \boldsymbol{0}\) is not guaranteed to hold for finite values of \(n\) without additional assumptions (such as the continuity of \(\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\)). This theoretical difficulty could be taken into account by defining \(1/\boldsymbol{Y}_{n}\) by some arbitrary way without any effect on the presented result on convergence in distribution. As is usual in the literary, this issue is not taken into account explicitly. A similar interpretation can be made in the case of Corollary 2 (iii).

The above results together with the mean value theorem can be used to prove the delta method, which is in the real valued case as follows. Let \(\theta \in \Theta\) be the parameter of interest and \(\theta_{0}\) its “true” value. Assume for simplicity that the parameter space \(\Theta\) is an open interval. Consider an asymptotically normal estimator \(\widehat{\theta}_{n}\) for \(\theta_{0}\), i.e., \[\begin{equation*} \sqrt{n}(\widehat{\theta }_{n}-\theta _{0})\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\mathsf{N}(0,\sigma ^{2}(\theta _{0})), \quad \left( \sigma^{2}(\theta _{0})>0\right) \end{equation*}\] If \(h:\Theta \rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) is continuously differentiable and \(h^{\prime}(\theta _{0})\neq 0\), then for the estimator \(h(\widehat{\theta}_{n})\) of the transformation \(h(\theta_{0})\) it holds that \[\begin{equation*} \sqrt{n}(h(\widehat{\theta }_{n})-h(\theta _{0}))\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\mathsf{N}(0,\left[ h(\theta _{0})\right]^{2}\sigma ^{2}(\theta _{0})) \end{equation*}\]

The results can be generalized to the vector case as follows. Let \(\Theta \subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\) be an open and convex set and \(h:\Theta \rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) a continuously differentiable function. Assume that the gradient vector \(\partial h(\boldsymbol{\theta} )/\partial \boldsymbol{\theta} ^{\prime }=[\partial h(\boldsymbol{\theta} )/\partial\theta _{1}\ \cdots \ \partial h(\boldsymbol{\theta} )/\partial \theta _{d}]\) satisfies \(\partial h(\boldsymbol{\theta} _{0})/\partial \boldsymbol{\theta} \neq \boldsymbol{0}\) and that for the estimator \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta }}_{n}\ (d\times 1)\) it holds that \[\begin{equation*} \sqrt{n}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta }}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta }_{0})\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma} (\boldsymbol{\theta }_{0})),\ \ \ \boldsymbol{\Sigma} (\boldsymbol{\theta }_{0})\text{ is positive definite} \end{equation*}\] Then, \[\begin{equation*} \sqrt{n}(h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta }}_{n})-h(\boldsymbol{\theta }_{0}))\overset{d}{\rightarrow }\mathsf{N}(0,V(\boldsymbol{\theta }_{0})),\ \ V(\boldsymbol{\theta }_{0})=\left[ \partial h(\boldsymbol{\theta} )/\partial\boldsymbol{\theta} ^{\prime }\right] \boldsymbol{\Sigma} (\boldsymbol{\theta }_{0})\left[ \partial h(\boldsymbol{\theta})/\partial \boldsymbol{\theta} \right] \end{equation*}\] The delta method can generalized further to a situation where \(\boldsymbol{h}\) is vector valued. In this case, the mean value theorem must be applied separately to each component of \(\boldsymbol{h}\).
